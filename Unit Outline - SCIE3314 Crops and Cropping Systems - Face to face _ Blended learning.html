Academic Information

Content This unit focuses on how agricultural systems in Western Australia are rapidly changing in response to the need for more environmentally responsible production methods and a changing climate. The unit introduces the main temperate crop species and then uses a systems approach to provide an understanding of the relationships between crops, pastures, livestock, weeds, plant diseases and the environment. There is in-depth consideration of crop agronomy, conservation cropping, no-tillage and weed and disease control. Modelling as a tool to improve our understanding of complex agricultural systems is discussed and models evaluated.

Unit Information

Unit Information
SCIE3314 Crops and Cropping Systems provides a comprehensive grounding in the principles and practice of broadacre grain production in Western Australia. The unit is a core requirement for students enrolled in Agriculture-related programs and assumes prior knowledge of plant biology and agricultural science fundamentals.
Western Australia operates within one of the world's most distinctive grain-producing environments — a Mediterranean-type climate with highly variable winter rainfall, ancient and characteristically infertile soils, and a compressed growing season that demands precise management timing. The unit takes this environment as its primary reference system, treating individual crop agronomy within a whole-farm systems context. Topics span the full cropping year: from paddock preparation, soil fertility, and variety selection through to crop protection, harvest management, farm financial analysis, and precision agriculture.
A defining feature of this unit is the integration of quantitative crop modelling. Students work with an industry-standard, process-based crop simulation model — accessed through a series of structured computer laboratory sessions — to connect theoretical concepts in soil water, nitrogen cycling, and crop phenology to practical farm management decisions. Modelling skills developed in these sessions underpin two separate assessment tasks.
The unit also incorporates two experiential learning components not available in a standard lecture format. First, student groups grow cereal crops in a supervised glasshouse trial on campus, managing a real experiment in parallel with a calibrated computer simulation of the same system. Second, the class engages with a commercial grain farming enterprise in the WA grainbelt through a structured diagnostic field visit and follow-up interview, with students taking on the role of specialist agronomic consultants.
On completion of SCIE3314 Crops and Cropping Systems, you will be able to:
LO1 — Global and Australian Context (Comprehension) Explain the role of Australian and Western Australian grain production within global food supply systems, and identify the environmental, economic, and social factors that shape the structure and management of broadacre cropping enterprises.
LO2 — Climate and Environment (Analysis) Analyse how Mediterranean-type climate patterns, soil constraints, and seasonal water balance dynamics in WA interact to determine crop management timing, variety selection, and production risk.
LO3 — Crop Agronomy and Systems (Application) Apply principles of conservation agriculture, soil fertility management, cereal phenology, and break-crop agronomy to develop management recommendations appropriate to the conditions and constraints of WA broadacre farming systems.
LO4 — Crop Protection (Evaluation) Assess the biological and economic impact of weeds, fungal diseases, insect pests, and soil-borne pathogens on grain production in WA, and formulate integrated management strategies that account for resistance mechanisms, economic thresholds, and environmental conditions.
LO5 — Quantitative Modelling (Application + Analysis) Demonstrate competence in using a process-based crop simulation model to predict crop growth, water use, and nitrogen response under varying management and climate scenarios, and critically evaluate model outputs to inform agronomic decision-making.
LO6 — Professional Agronomic Synthesis (Synthesis) Synthesise agronomic, environmental, financial, and crop protection knowledge to construct a professional agronomic management package for a major WA grain crop, including a gross margin analysis and a quantitative nitrogen management strategy.

Learning And Teaching Strategy

This unit employs a layered, experience-led approach in which theoretical knowledge introduced in lectures is progressively reinforced through computational modelling, supervised practical work, and professional field engagement. The overall design mirrors the workflow of a practising agronomist: build conceptual understanding, develop a quantitative model, test it against real data, and translate findings into professional recommendations.
- Lectures and online content
Two hours of lectures are scheduled each week, covering thirteen content themes across the semester. Lectures are delivered by the unit coordinator and are complemented by contributions from specialist guest academics and industry professionals, who bring current research and commercial perspectives to topics including conservation agriculture, crop diseases, insect ecology, and farm business management. Several topics — particularly those requiring specialist expertise that may not be available each year in the same format — are delivered as pre-recorded online sessions accessible through the LMS page. Students are expected to view these recordings independently before the associated in-class discussion or tutorial session.
- Crop modelling computer laboratory sessions
A series of structured computer laboratory sessions in the first half of semester introduces students to an industry-standard, process-based crop simulation model. Sessions are sequentially scaffolded, progressing from basic simulation configuration and soil water analysis through to nitrogen management, crop phenology, climate risk assessment, and model calibration against observed data. Students bring their own laptop with the relevant software pre-installed; setup instructions are provided on LMS before Week 1. The competencies developed in these sessions directly underpin two separate assessment tasks. Beyond the initial modelling series, further computer laboratory sessions later in the semester introduce decision-support tools for weed population dynamics and farm financial analysis, broadening students' evidence-based decision-making toolkit.
- Glasshouse pot trial — group practical component
Student trios manage a live cereal crop trial in a supervised glasshouse facility on campus across the first portion of the teaching semester. Each group applies a budget-constrained management strategy — controlling nitrogen, water, and diagnostic inputs — while conducting structured, twice-weekly measurements of crop growth and development. Groups simultaneously run and calibrate a computer simulation as a predictive digital twin of their experiment, and compete on a live leaderboard based on resource use efficiency and model prediction accuracy. This component develops skills in experimental management, systematic data collection, model calibration, and evidence-based group decision-making.
- Commercial farm engagement — diagnostic field visit
The unit links classroom learning to professional practice through a structured engagement with a commercial grain farming enterprise in the WA grainbelt. Early in the semester, students travel to a working grain farm where they conduct a structured diagnostic interview with the farm management team, working in specialist groups covering areas such as crop protection, soil and plant nutrition, financial analysis, weather and climate, mechanised operations, and human resource management. A follow-up interview is conducted later in the semester. Information gathered across both visits underpins a group diagnostic report and provides the site-specific empirical data for the individual Agronomic Package report. The group diagnostic report includes a peer assessment component; detailed criteria and grade weightings are specified in the Assessment section of this outline.
- Online self-paced modules
A set of accredited online modules on weed management — covering topics such as crop-weed competition, harvest weed seed control, herbicide resistance, and pre-emergent herbicides — are completed independently by each student during the first half of the teaching semester. Completion of these modules contributes to an individual assessment task. Enrolment links and instructions are provided through LMS at the start of semester.
- Staff and student responsibilities
Staff are responsible for delivering content that is current, well-referenced, and grounded in WA farming systems; for providing timely formative feedback on the group diagnostic report before the individual Agronomic Package report is due; and for maintaining the experimental competition leaderboard and making class-wide monitoring data accessible through LMS.
Students are expected to: attend all timetabled Tuesday and Thursday sessions; complete designated online content and pre-readings before the relevant contact session; install and test required modelling software before the first week of teaching; fulfil their responsibilities to their glasshouse trial partners, including self-scheduled monitoring visits; and contribute equitably to their specialist diagnostic group at the farm visit. The balance between individual and group assessment responsibilities is detailed in the Assessment section.

Unit Structure

eaching weeks and contact hours
The unit runs for twelve teaching weeks, with a mid-semester non-teaching break approximately halfway through the semester. Each week has four scheduled contact hours, delivered in two two-hour blocks — one on Tuesday afternoon and one on Thursday afternoon. The full timetable, including room allocations, is available on the UWA Timetabling System (CAS) and on LMS.
Tuesday sessions — Lectures
Tuesday sessions are held in a lecture theatre and cover the unit's substantive content themes. The lecture programme draws on the unit coordinator and a rotating programme of specialist guest contributors from WA agricultural research institutions and industry. In weeks where online lectures have been pre-assigned, the Tuesday session may be used for structured discussion, tutorials, or problem-based activities rather than new lecture delivery.
Thursday sessions — Computer Laboratory and Lectures
Thursday sessions alternate between a dedicated undergraduate computer laboratory and the lecture theatre, depending on the week's topic and activities. Students must check the LMS schedule at the start of each week to confirm the Thursday venue.
In the first half of the teaching semester, Thursday sessions are held in the computer laboratory and focus on the sequential crop modelling series. The six sessions progress as follows:
Session 1: Configuring and running basic crop simulations
Session 2: Nitrogen management and nitrogen response analysis
Session 3: Seasonal climate inputs and forecasting tools
Session 4: Crop ecophysiology — phenology and biomass
Session 5: Rotation effects and water use efficiency
Session 6: Model calibration and data analysis
In the second half of the teaching semester, Thursday sessions continue to use the computer laboratory for applied software exercises — including weed population modelling and farm investment analysis tools — before returning to lecture-format delivery in the final teaching weeks.
Glasshouse trial — pot trial programme
The supervised glasshouse trial begins in the first week of teaching. Twice-weekly monitoring visits to the on-campus glasshouse facility are conducted by each student trio throughout approximately the first ten teaching weeks. These visits are self-scheduled by each group around formal timetabled sessions; the schedule is confirmed between group members and the unit coordinator at the start of semester.
Out-of-class compulsory activities
Three structured activities outside the formal timetable form part of the assessed programme:
Diagnostic field visit: A whole-of-class visit to a commercial grain farming enterprise in the WA grainbelt, typically held on a weekend day in the first half of semester. Students work in specialist diagnostic groups and conduct a structured interview with farm management. Transport arrangements and logistical details are confirmed on LMS no later than the first week of teaching.
Follow-up farm interview: A second structured engagement with the same enterprise, incorporated into the timetabled schedule in the second half of the semester.
Agricultural industry facility visit: Scheduled in the final teaching weeks. This visit may involve travel to an off-campus location; details are confirmed on LMS.
Online components
Several content modules — particularly relating to crop disease epidemiology and management — are delivered as pre-recorded online sessions and are expected to be completed independently before scheduled discussion sessions. Accredited external online modules on integrated weed management are also completed independently as part of an individual assessment task. All online resources are accessible through LMS.
Non-teaching week
No classes are held during the mid-semester non-teaching break. Students are encouraged to use this period to advance their modelling work, review their glasshouse trial data against simulation predictions, and begin gathering material for their Agronomic Package report.

Late submission of Assignments

A penalty of 5 per cent of the total mark allocated for the assessment item is deducted per day for the first 7 days (including weekends and public holidays) after which the assignment is not accepted.

For the first two days there will be a penalty waiver, which means that if you submit within that 48 hours period, the assessment will be marked late but the late penalty will not be applied. Please note: group assignments are exempt from 48 hour waiver

After 48 hours, the accrued penalty will apply, i.e. a 15% deduction will be applied on Day 3 (after 48 hours), with an additional 5% per day after that (to day 7).

Penalty for exceeding word limit

Where a submitted assignment exceeds the word limit, a penalty of 1 per cent of the total mark allocated for the assessment task applies for each 1 per cent in excess of the word limit, or the marking ceases once the word limit is reached.
