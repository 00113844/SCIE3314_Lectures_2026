Part 1

Why Cereals Dominate the WA Grainbelt

Reliability, market depth, and the risk gradient that explains why rotations look different at Merredin than at Esperance.

Target: ~15 min for this part. Preview: first the descriptive fact (cereals are most of the area), then the complementary roles of the two crops, then the argument that answers the learning outcome head-on — why cereals are less risky than break crops — and finally the consequence for rotation geography. Wheat and Barley: the Backbone of WA Cropping

  • Together they occupy the majority of the WA cropped area and generate most of the region's grain export income.
  • Adapted to the Mediterranean pattern — winter-growing, spring-finishing, matched to a cool wet season and a dry finish.
  • Establish reliably, even from a marginal or late break.
  • Tolerate poor, variable and constrained soils better than the alternatives.
  • Deep, liquid export markets with well-understood specifications.
  • Mature support system — agronomy, breeding, machinery and advice all built around them.
Target: ~3 min. Open on the photo, not the bullets: this is the default landscape of the grainbelt — cereal sown into retained stubble, no-till, the system L01 and the conservation-agriculture material described. Make the point that "dominant" is not an accident of habit; each bullet is a genuine agronomic or economic advantage, and the rest of the lecture unpacks the middle two (reliable establishment, stress tolerance) as physiology. Anticipate the obvious student question — "if break crops are more valuable, why not grow more of them?" — and promise it is answered two slides from now. Connect back to L04–L05: the Mediterranean pattern named there is exactly what these crops are matched to. The number that reframes the whole unit, and worth putting up early: roughly 90% of WA grain is exported. Almost nothing grown here is eaten here. That single fact explains a great deal of what follows — why grain is bought against published specifications rather than by negotiation, why WA specialises into particular Asian classes such as the noodle wheats, why "nil tolerance" insect standards apply in storage, and why Part 5 spends twenty minutes on quality parameters that a domestic-market system could afford to treat loosely. A grower here is not selling food; they are filling a container against a contract.

Two Crops, Complementary Roles

Wheat

  • The higher-value milling staple and the rotational anchor — the crop the system is built around.
  • Graded into protein-based quality classes that reward nitrogen management.
  • WA grows spring wheats: little or no vernalisation requirement, low photoperiod sensitivity, suited to a short season.
  • Slightly longer season and less salt-tolerant than barley.

Barley

  • Faster and more robust — often more drought- and salt-tolerant, and better suited to the tougher country.
  • Takes the later sowing slots when the break is late.
  • Two markets: a malting premium against exacting specifications, or feed with looser ones.
  • WA grows almost exclusively two-rowed spring barley.
Target: ~3 min. The framing to land: these are not competing choices so much as complementary tools — wheat is what you grow when you can, barley is what you grow when the paddock or the calendar is against you, and also what you grow deliberately when you want a shot at the malting premium. The two-rowed / six-rowed distinction is worth 30 seconds: both types have three florets per node, but in two-rowed barley only the central floret is fertile, which is why two-rowed grain is plumper and more even — and plumpness is exactly what the maltster is buying. That is the reason WA grows two-rowed almost exclusively. Flag the spring-versus-winter habit point too, because it recurs in the phenology section: winter types need vernalisation and a 9–10 month season; WA's season is 6–7 months, so spring types dominate here while winter types are used in Tasmania and Victoria. Give them the WA geography and the pace of change, because both surprise students. Barley production concentrates in Agzones 2 and 3, Agzone 2 being the largest producing region — barley is not evenly spread across the belt, it clusters where the soils and the finish suit it. And WA growers turn varieties over remarkably fast: La Trobe was about 30% of WA barley production in 2017 and has since been displaced, with Spartacus surging in behind it (it returned 104% of site mean yield in 2018 trials). Draw the lesson rather than the list: a variety table has a shelf life of about three years, so what students need to carry out of this unit is the method for evaluating a variety against a site and a market, not the names on today's slide.

Why Cereals Are Less Risky Than Break Crops

  1. 1Establishment reliability. Large seed, vigorous coleoptile, tolerant of a wide range of sowing conditions. Canola and pulses have small seed and narrower requirements, so a dry start is far more likely to cost the whole stand.
  2. 2Stress tolerance and compensation. Cereals tolerate drought, heat and nutrient stress better, and — crucially — compensate for early stress through tillering. Canola and pulses are much less forgiving.
  3. 3Market depth and price stability. Wheat and barley trade into large, liquid, well-understood markets. Pulse and even canola markets are thinner and more volatile.
  4. 4Agronomic maturity. Weed, disease and nutrition programs for cereals are well established, well supported and comparatively low-risk to execute.
Target: ~4 min. This slide answers a unit learning outcome directly — say that out loud. Work through the four reasons but weight them: reasons 1 and 2 are biological and are the ones we will explain properly later today; reasons 3 and 4 are economic and institutional. The sharpest way to put reason 2 for a third-year audience: a cereal that loses half its plants in a poor establishment will tiller harder and may still make a respectable head count, whereas a canola crop that loses half its plants has simply lost half its plants. That is a difference in architecture, not in toughness — and the architecture is what the next part of the lecture is about. Ask the room to hold the question: "if cereals are so much safer, why grow anything else?" The answer — disease and weed breaks, nitrogen, and higher gross margins in the right season — comes on the next slide and returns in L07/L08.

The Risk Gradient Explains Rotation Geography

Across the WA grainbelt, rotation composition tracks rainfall — and therefore risk:

  • Low-rainfall, high-risk east (e.g. Merredin, Mullewa): growers lean heavily on cereals and use break crops sparingly and strategically. Rotations here optimise for maximum profit, not maximum diversity.
  • Higher-rainfall, lower-risk south and west (e.g. Esperance, Great Southern): the more valuable but riskier break crops — canola, lupins, pulses — can safely occupy a larger share.
  • The break crops still earn their place everywhere: disease and weed breaks, and nitrogen. Wheat after lupins shows Take-all incidence falling from about 54% to roughly 15–19%.
Target: ~3 min. The idea to land is that the map of WA rotations is a map of risk tolerance. A grower in a 300 mm zone is not being unadventurous by running long cereal sequences — they are making a rational allocation under a distribution with a fat lower tail, exactly the yield-distribution thinking from L01. Then complicate it immediately with the Take-all number: the cereal-heavy rotation carries a hidden cost that compounds over years, so "lean on cereals" is not the same as "grow only cereals". This tension — short-run risk versus long-run system health — is the theme of L13 (crop rotation) and returns in L08 when we make the case for lupins. Do not resolve it here; leave it open.
A grower at Merredin and a grower at Esperance both know that canola pays more than wheat. They plant different rotations anyway. What are they each optimising for — and what would have to change for them to swap?
Target: ~2 min. Run this as a genuine 60-second turn-and-talk, then take two or three answers. Answers to steer toward: the Merredin grower is optimising the lower tail (avoiding a failed establishment and a total loss in a dry year), the Esperance grower can optimise the mean because their lower tail is much shallower. What would have to change: a more reliable break (better establishment technology, a shorter-season canola), a bigger price premium to compensate for the variance, or a worsening disease/resistance burden that makes continuous cereal untenable — which is precisely what herbicide-resistant ryegrass is doing across the belt (L09). Use this to bridge into Part 2: to make the risk argument properly we need the architecture of the plant.

Part 2

The Cereal Plant and Its Yield Components

The architecture that makes compensation possible, and the multiplicative chain that lets you attribute a yield loss to a date.

Target: ~15 min for this part. Preview: the vegetative body (tillers, leaves, the flag leaf), then the reproductive structure (spike, spikelet, floret), then the yield-component chain that connects the two, and finally the sequencing of when each component is fixed. Tell students this is the most conceptually loaded stretch of the lecture and that everything after the break refers back to it.

The Cereal Plant: Vegetative Architecture

  • A main stem plus tillers — side shoots from the crown, each one a potential head.
  • Stems are built of nodes and internodes; the swollen nodes you can feel through the sheath are how stem elongation is scored.
  • Each leaf has a blade, a sheath, and at their junction a ligule and auricles — the ligule is the marker for "fully emerged" when counting leaves.
  • The topmost leaf is the flag leaf; it and the ear supply most of the carbohydrate that fills the grain.
  • Below ground: seminal roots from the seed, then nodal roots from the crown once the crown establishes at a consistent depth.
Source: DPIRD/GRDC Wheat Book: Principles and Practice, ch. 2
Target: ~3 min. This is vocabulary, but it is vocabulary with a purpose: every term on this slide appears in a growth-stage definition later. Ligule → "fully emerged leaf" for Z1x scoring. Node → "first node detectable" for Z31. Tiller → Z2x. Flag leaf → Z39 and the fungicide decision. Say that explicitly so students see the anatomy as the index to the staging scale rather than as botany for its own sake. The crown deserves a moment: it forms at a fairly consistent shallow depth regardless of sowing depth, and the sub-crown internode extends to put it there — which is why sowing depth costs the seedling energy rather than simply burying the plant. That sets up the sowing depth photograph in Part 3.

Where the Grain Actually Sits: Ear, Spikelet, Floret

Wheat inflorescence: ear → spikelet (on the rachis) → floret (lemma, palea, anthers, ovary). Roughly 18–22 spikelets per head, 1–5 florets per spikelet, and typically 30–50 grains per head.

Source: DPIRD/GRDC Wheat Book: Principles and Practice, ch. 2
Target: ~3 min. Walk the magnification series left to right and make the arithmetic explicit, because it is the arithmetic that matters: 18–22 spikelets, up to 5 florets each — so a head has on the order of 80–100 potential grain sites but delivers 30–50. The gap between potential and delivered sites is where flowering-time stress does its damage, and it is a much bigger lever than most students expect. Point out the anthers specifically: they are how anthesis is scored in the field (Z61 first anthers at mid-ear, Z65 half way, Z69 complete), and their exposure is why heat above about 35 °C at flowering is so destructive — pollen is the vulnerable tissue. Barley contrast worth 30 seconds: three florets at every node, but in the two-rowed types WA grows only the central floret is fertile, hence the two neat rows of large even grain.

Yield Components: the Multiplicative Chain

  1. 1Plants per m² — set at establishment. Driven by seed rate, seedbed moisture, sowing depth, seed size and pests.
  2. 2× Heads per plant — set during tillering and tiller survival. Driven by nitrogen, moisture, light (density) and temperature.
  3. 3× Grains per head — set from stem elongation to flowering. Cut by frost, heat, water and nutrient stress.
  4. 4× Weight per grain — set during grain fill, by assimilate supply against the duration of filling. Typically 30–40 mg in a kind finish.
150 plants/m² × 3 heads × 30 grains × 35 mg ≈ 4.7 t/ha.  Hold everything else and drop grains per head to 20, and the same crop makes 3.2 t/ha. The chain is multiplicative, so proportional losses compound — and each link is forged in a different fortnight.
Target: ~5 min. This framework traces to Engledow and Wadham and it underpins all cereal agronomy and every crop model in the unit. Two things to stress. First, it is multiplicative: a component driven to zero drives yield to zero, and proportional changes compound. Do the arithmetic on the board — 150 plants/m² × 3 heads × 30 grains × 35 mg is about 4.7 t/ha; drop grains per head to 20 and you are at 3.2 t/ha with everything else unchanged. Second, and this is the point of the whole framework: because each component is fixed in a different window, an agronomist who knows which component is short can name the fortnight in which the yield was lost. That is diagnosis, and it is exactly what the crop- diagnosis material later in the unit formalises. Ask: "you cut a crop at harvest and find plenty of heads but only 18 grains per head — when did this crop have its bad week?" (Answer: somewhere between Z31 and flowering.)

The Components Are Set Sequentially — and They Overlap

Tiller number peaks and then self-thins; floret number peaks and is then heavily aborted around flowering; grain weight accumulates only after flowering. The crop is continuously revising its own yield target as resources allow.

Source: DPIRD/GRDC Wheat Book: Principles and Practice, ch. 3
Target: ~4 min. This is the single most important diagram in the lecture — give it time. Three features to point at. (1) Tiller number overshoots then falls. The crop deliberately produces more tillers than it can support and then aborts the late ones when resources tighten at stem elongation. That is not failure; it is the crop matching head number to the season it is actually getting. (2) Floret number does the same thing on a shorter timescale and with much bigger stakes: the crop initiates far more florets than it sets grains, and the abortion happens right at flowering. (3) Grain weight only starts at flowering, which is why it is the last component available to compensate and why a kind finish matters so much. The synthesis: this overshoot-then-abort architecture is the physiological machinery behind the "cereals are less risky" claim from Part 1. The crop is built to revise its ambitions downward gracefully. Canola and pulses have far less of this machinery. End the pre-break segment here and set up the break by saying: "after the break we put dates on this diagram."

What Actually Drives Development

  • Four things, together, fix the number of days between germination and flowering:
  • Thermal time — accumulated temperature. The engine; always running.
  • Vernalisation (Vrn) — a requirement for a period of cold before the plant will commit to flowering.
  • Photoperiod (Ppd) — a long-day response; the plant waits for the days to lengthen.
  • Earliness per se — the inherent varietal differences left over once Vrn and Ppd are accounted for.
  • Genotype enters through named genes — VRN1, PPD1 — whose allele combinations are what make one variety quick and another slow.
The WA case. Varieties such as Mace and Scepter are quick spring types: little vernalisation requirement, low photoperiod sensitivity. Their development is driven almost entirely by thermal time. Winter wheats and long-spring types will not commit to flowering until they have had cold, long days, or both.
Target: ~3 min. This slide is the genetic and physiological basis of the single highest-leverage decision in cereal agronomy, so do not rush it — everything later in Part 3 about timing assumes students know what sets the clock. The framing that makes it stick: the crop is not counting days, it is satisfying conditions. Thermal time is the one that always accumulates; vernalisation and photoperiod are gates that can hold a plant in the vegetative phase no matter how warm it gets. A winter wheat sown in WA in May without adequate cold simply will not flower properly — not late, but wrong. Introduce earliness per se carefully, because the name is unhelpful. It is the residual: two varieties with identical vernalisation and photoperiod responses can still differ in time to heading, and breeders use that residual for fine adjustment. It is how you get a family of varieties that all suit WA but spread flowering across three weeks. Then the practical payoff, which is worth saying explicitly: variety and sowing date are one decision, not two. The same sowing date is early for a slow variety and late for a quick one. Growers choose the pair that lands flowering inside the window between the last damaging frost and the onset of terminal drought — and in WA that window is narrow, which is why quick spring types dominate. Flag forward: this is the phenology an APSIM run is simulating when it predicts a flowering date, which is the L02 material arriving with a physiological basis underneath it.

Thermal Time: How the Crop Keeps Time

  • Every phase of development requires a minimum accumulation of temperature before it can complete and the plant can move on.
  • In effect the plant senses temperature each day and adds it to a running total. That total is thermal time, or the heat sum, measured in degree-days (°Cd).
  • Each day contributes the mean temperature less a base temperature — about 5 °C for wheat, below which development effectively stops.

So a warm week advances the crop further than a cool week of the same length. Two crops sown on the same date in different seasons reach Z30 on different dates — which is precisely why every chemical label is written in growth stages and never in weeks after sowing.
Target: ~3 min. Do the arithmetic on the board with the room, slowly. It is the only calculation in this lecture and students remember the concept far better once they have done one by hand. Walk it: take a day, 35 °C max and 15 °C min. Mean is 25. Subtract the 5 °C base, because below about 5 °C a wheat plant is not developing in any useful sense. That day banked 20 °Cd. Do the same for every day of a phase and total it — that is the heat sum for the phase. Anticipate the two questions that always come. First: "is the base really 5?" It is a working approximation and it differs by species and by phase; the principle is what matters. Second: "what about a 40 °C day?" Above an optimum, development rate stops increasing and heat starts doing damage instead — the simple linear sum is a model, and like all models it has a domain. Say so; it is good practice for L02. The payoff to land: this is why the calendar is a poor guide and staging is not. A crop sown on the same day in a warm season and a cool season is at genuinely different growth stages in mid August, and the spray that is correct for one is off-label for the other. Connect forward to the thermal-time figure later in this part — the horizontal axis on that chart runs to about 3100 °Cd, and students should now know exactly what that number is counting.

The Zadoks Scale: a Common Language

How the code works

  • Two digits. The first is the principal growth stage; the second subdivides it.
  • Z13 = three leaves unfolded. Z24 = main stem plus four tillers. Z30 = start of stem elongation. Z39 = flag leaf ligule just visible. Z65 = anthesis half way.
  • Field-observable. Every definition is something you can see or feel on a plant you have pulled — no laboratory required.
  • Conventions matter — and there are four of them that catch people out. Two slides on, we do them properly.

Why it matters

  • Nearly every agronomic decision is specified by stage, not by date — because the crop's physiological readiness, not the calendar, determines the response.
  • It is the label language: chemical registrations are written in growth stages.
  • It is the bridge to crop models — APSIM predicts phenology in Zadoks terms, and staging is how you check the model against the paddock.
Source: DPIRD/GRDC Wheat Book: Principles and Practice, ch. 2
Target: ~3 min. Frame Zadoks as an interface rather than a taxonomy: it is the agreed vocabulary that lets a chemical registrant in Sydney, an agronomist at Katanning and a model running in a laptop all refer to the same moment in a crop's life. Do not work the conventions here — they get a slide of their own shortly, with a worked scoring example. Just plant the flag that the scale has rules, and that the rules are where students lose marks and agronomists lose crops. Note the near-universal alternative notation students will meet in industry: GS31 and Z31 mean the same thing.

The Ten Principal Stages

Vegetative

  • Z0x Germination
  • Z1x Seedling growth — count leaves on the main stem
  • Z2x Tillering — count tillers on the main stem

Reproductive

  • Z3x Stem elongation — count detectable nodes
  • Z4x Booting
  • Z5x Ear emergence
  • Z6x Anthesis (flowering)

Grain fill & ripening

  • Z7x Milk development
  • Z8x Dough development
  • Z9x Ripening
Second digits are not arbitrary. In Z1x and Z2x the second digit is a literal count (Z13 = 3 leaves; Z24 = 4 tillers). In Z3x it counts detectable nodes, with Z30 and Z39 reserved for "leaf sheath erect" and "flag leaf ligule visible". In Z5x–Z9x it is a fraction or a texture description.
Target: ~2 min. Do not read the list — students can. Instead teach the pattern, because the pattern is what makes the scale memorable: the second digit means "how many" during the counting stages and "how far through" during the emergence and filling stages. Once students see that, they stop memorising and start reading. Two practical warnings. First, leaf counting only ever happens on the main stem, and a leaf counts as emerged when its ligule is visible — the anatomy slide from Part 2. Second, the sub-stage can be fractional in the literature (Z13.4 = two emerged leaves plus a third four-tenths out), which is more precision than a paddock decision usually needs but appears in research papers and in the reference figures on the next slide.

The Whole Sequence, and the Actions Keyed to It

Same axis, top and bottom. Development runs on thermal time (degree-days), not calendar days — and every management action is pinned to a stage on that axis, never to a date.

Target: ~3 min. Use this to make the underlying driver explicit. The x-axis is not days, it is accumulated degree-days, and that single fact explains most of what is puzzling about phenology: a warm start pulls every stage forward, a cold one pushes it back, and the northern grainbelt (Geraldton) runs through these stages faster than the south (Borden) because it accumulates thermal time faster. Point out the two structural features worth remembering: (1) the phases overlap heavily — tillering is still running while stem elongation begins, and stem storage is laid down before flowering ready for remobilisation later; (2) roughly two-thirds of the thermal time is spent before flowering (GS61) and only the last third fills the grain, which is exactly why a truncated finish is so costly. Reinforce that vernalisation and photoperiod modify this clock — but WA's spring types have little vernalisation requirement and low photoperiod sensitivity, so thermal time does most of the work here.

Reading the Scale: the Rules That Catch People Out

  1. 1The scale is not chronological. A plant with three fully formed leaves has already begun to tiller — it is at Z20 — before leaves 4 to 6 exist. The two digits are a code, not a timeline.
  2. 2Once tillering starts, count tillers, not leaves. The lower leaves senesce and the leaf count stops being recoverable, so tiller number becomes the reliable observation.
  3. 3Different parts of one plant sit at different stages. Score the most advanced. A plant with 2 nodes, 3 tillers and 7 leaves is Z32, Z23 and Z17 simultaneously — you record Z32.
  4. 4From Z30 the score describes the main stem, not an average of the tillers. Before Z30 you are describing a plant; after Z30 you are describing one stem on it.
Field technique for Z30–Z33. Node position cannot be judged from the outside. Cut the stem lengthways and look: the nodes are solid, the internodes hollow, and the count of nodes that have moved up from the base gives you the second digit. This is the stage that governs both nitrogen timing and the fungicide decision, so it is worth doing properly.

Germination and Emergence: the Sowing-Depth Trade-off

  • Germination and emergence are driven by seedbed moisture and temperature; this phase sets the plants per m² component.

Target: ~3 min. Let the photograph do the argument — it is unusually clear. Tie back to Part 1: this reliable, vigorous establishment from a wide range of conditions is precisely the advantage cereals hold over canola, whose much smaller seed has almost none of this latitude.

Tillering: the First Great Compensation Mechanism

  • From about the three-leaf stage the plant produces tillers from the crown — each one a potential head.
  • Tiller number responds strongly to conditions: moisture, nitrogen, light (low density) and warmth promote tillering; stress suppresses it.
  • The consequence is a buffer: a thin or stressed crop tillers harder to fill the canopy; a thick or vigorous crop makes fewer tillers per plant. Head number is stabilised across a wide range of plant densities.
  • Not all tillers survive. Late tillers are aborted around stem elongation if resources tighten — a self-thinning that matches head number to the season on offer.
  • Agronomically: early nitrogen and early weed control protect tillering and canopy establishment.
Source: DPIRD/GRDC Wheat Book: Principles and Practice, ch. 3
Target: ~3 min. This is the mechanism the whole risk argument rests on, so slow down. Make the buffering concrete with numbers: at 150 plants/m² each plant might carry three heads for 450 heads/m²; at 80 plants/m² the same crop may carry five or six heads per plant and land at 400–480. The head count — the component that actually matters — barely moves. That is why a cereal survives a patchy establishment and a canola crop does not. Then flag the cost, because students should not leave thinking tillering is free: a crop that compensates by tillering is later and more variable in maturity than one that established evenly, and the late tillers that do survive produce smaller heads and smaller grain — which shows up as screenings at delivery. Compensation preserves tonnes more faithfully than it preserves quality. Park that; it returns in Part 5. Floral Initiation: the Decision You Cannot See

  • Somewhere around Z29/Z30, just before stem elongation, the shoot apex stops producing leaves and starts producing spikelets.
  • The marker is the double ridge — the paired ridges visible on the apex in the plate. Note the scale: it is about half a millimetre across.
  • It happens at ground level, inside the crown. Nothing on the outside of the plant announces it. It is found only by dissection under magnification.
  • And it is where spikelet number per head is set — the second term of the yield chain — weeks before there is a head to look at.
So the crop commits to a head size before it commits to a stem. Stress in the run-up to Z30 costs spikelets, and no later kindness recovers them: the compensation available afterwards is grains per spikelet and grain weight, not spikelets. Equally, nitrogen and water in this window are buying potential grain sites.

Target: ~2 min. This slide exists to make one uncomfortable point: the most consequential thing happening in the paddock in early August is invisible from the ute. Set it up against the yield-component chain from Part 2. Students have the sequence — plants, heads, grains per head, grain weight — and they know each is set at a particular time. This is the moment the third term begins to be determined, and it is determined at a scale of half a millimetre, underground, weeks before anything is visible. The practical consequence is the one to press: an agronomist walking a crop at Z25 and seeing a healthy canopy cannot see that a nitrogen or moisture deficit is at that moment costing spikelets on every head the crop will ever produce. Stem Elongation: the Growing Point Comes Up

From Z30 the growing point, previously protected at ground level, rises up the stem. It is now exposed to frost and to several herbicide chemistries — which is why Z30/Z31 is a hard cut-off on many labels.

Source: DPIRD/GRDC Wheat Book: Principles and Practice, ch. 3
Target: ~3 min. The mechanical story first: before Z30 the developing head sits at or below ground level inside the crown, insulated and protected. From Z30 the internodes extend and carry it upward. Everything that follows is a consequence of that geometry. Consequence one — herbicide safety. Phenoxy chemistry is the standard example: 2,4-D amine in WA is applied after about the five-leaf stage (Z15/Z16) and before jointing; applied outside that window it distorts the developing head and produces the classic "onion leafing" symptom. This is not a precaution, it is a yield loss. Consequence two — frost exposure. The head is now above ground, and it stays exposed for the rest of the season. Consequence three — this is where grain sites are being determined, and where the crop decides which tillers to keep. Nitrogen applied around here is being converted into grains per head; nitrogen deficiency here loses tillers you had already paid for. Mention the double-ridge stage (visible only under a microscope) as the true switch from vegetative to reproductive — it happens well before anything is visible in the paddock, which is why models and not eyeballs are needed to predict flowering date.

Booting, Ear Emergence and Flowering

  • Booting (Z41–49): the head swells inside the flag-leaf sheath. Scored on the sheath — extending, swollen, splitting, first awns visible.
  • Ear emergence (Z51–59): scored as the fraction of the ear out of the boot.
  • Anthesis (Z61–69): scored on visible anthers — Z61 first anthers mid-ear, Z65 half way, Z69 complete.
  • This is the yield-defining event. Florets are pollinated and grain number is finally set.
  • The losses here are largely irreversible, because the most important yield component is fixed and there is nothing left to compensate with except grain size.
Target: ~3 min. Be blunt about the stakes: everything before this slide was the crop building capacity; this fortnight is where the capacity is converted into a committed grain number, and after it the crop can only fill what it has set. The three threats, in the order they bite in WA: Frost sterilises florets. A frost at Z65 can empty a head completely while leaving the plant looking green and healthy — the classic "white heads at harvest" that growers find weeks later. Heat above about 35 °C damages pollen directly; the anthers in this photograph are the vulnerable tissue. Water and nutrient stress reduce the number of grains the crop can commit to support. Hence the central strategic decision in WA cereal agronomy, which students have already met in the climate lecture: choose variety and sowing date so that flowering lands in the safe window — late enough to clear the frost risk, early enough to beat the heat and the terminal drought. That window is narrow, it moves with location, and by flowering it is far too late to change it. Every decision that determines it was made before sowing.

Grain Filling: the Flag Leaf Does the Work

  • After flowering the grain accumulates starch and protein through milk (Z7x) and dough (Z8x) to maturity (Z9x).
  • Two sources: current photosynthesis from the flag leaf and upper canopy, plus remobilised stem reserves laid down before flowering.
  • In wheat the flag leaf supplies about 43% of grain-fill carbohydrate — which is what makes a flag-leaf fungicide such a high-value decision.
  • In barley the picture differs: the flag leaf contributes only 5–9%, and the crop leans on leaf 2 (20–40%) and the leaf sheaths. Protect a different layer.
  • Grain size is set by the balance of assimilate supply against the duration of fill.

Grain Fill in WA: Filling Into a Terminal Drought

  • In a Mediterranean environment, grain fill almost always runs into rising heat and a drying profile. The fill period is truncated.
  • Grain is therefore frequently smaller than its genetic potential — the pinched, screened grain of a dry finish.
  • This costs both yield and quality: lower grain weight, lower test weight, higher screenings.
  • A kind finish — a September or October rain — can lift grain size and test weight markedly. Growers call it a "soft finish"; it is the single biggest determinant of plumpness.
  • Maturity (Z9x) is reached when grain dries to harvestable moisture. Harvest promptly: grain left standing is exposed to weather damage and sprouting.
Target: ~3 min. This slide is where the abstract "terminal drought" of the climate lecture becomes a physical grain you can hold. The photograph is the argument: same variety, same paddock, same season, at Z87 — the difference is a fungicide that kept the canopy alive for the last fortnight of fill. Reinforce the asymmetry that will run through Part 5: grain fill sets only grain size, the smallest of the four yield components, but it disproportionately sets quality. A crop can lose 10% of its yield in the finish and lose its milling grade entirely — and the grade is worth more than the 10%. On harvest timing: this is not an afterthought. Late-season rain on ripe standing grain triggers pre-harvest sprouting, raises alpha-amylase and collapses the falling number, which is a hard downgrade for milling and noodle wheats. Prompt harvest is a quality decision, and it links directly to the logistics and storage material in L13.

Why Growth Stage Governs Management

  • Herbicides. Safe and effective only inside a stage window. 2,4-D amine: after ~Z15, before jointing — outside it you get head distortion and "onion leafing".
  • Nitrogen. Topdressed from tillering through stem elongation, matched to the stage at which the crop can still convert N into heads and grains — and to the seasonal outlook.
  • Fungicides. Timed to protect a specific leaf layer at a defined stage — flag leaf in wheat, leaf 2 in barley.
  • Plant growth regulators. Applied at stem elongation to manage lodging.
  • Frost and heat risk. Assessed against flowering stage, and locked in by variety and sowing date months earlier.
Target: ~3 min. This is the consolidation slide for Part 3 — the practical payoff of everything since the break. State the principle in one line and make students write it down: the crop's response to an input depends on its developmental readiness, not on the date. Then push on the corollary that students find genuinely difficult: because the same variety reaches Z31 on different dates in different seasons and at different sites, a calendar-based program is wrong somewhere every year, and the error is largest in exactly the seasons where the margin is thinnest. Close on the field skill, which is the real learning outcome: a paddock is never at one stage. There is a spread across plants, across soil types within the paddock, and between main stems and late tillers. The agronomist's job is to sample properly, stage the main stems, understand the distribution, and decide which part of that distribution the decision is actually governed by — usually the most advanced material for a crop-safety call, and the bulk of the crop for an efficacy call.

The Same Stress at Different Stages Costs Different Amounts

Stress at pollination costs the most (kernel blanks, sterility — very high yield impact) and is the shortest window. Stress at tillering is moderate and the crop has a long time to recover.

Target: ~2 min. Read the table down the "yield impact" column and let the shape speak: moderate, high, moderate, very high, moderate-to-low. The peak is at pollination, and the window is short — which is the worst possible combination, because it means a few unlucky days can cost more than a month of moderate stress. Draw out the fourth column too, because it is the one that surprises: the quantity of water needed to avoid stress is low at tillering and high at pollination and grain fill. So the crop's water demand and its vulnerability to water shortage peak together, in exactly the part of the WA season when the profile is emptying. Two things follow that are worth saying explicitly. First, this is why the flowering-window decision made back at sowing is the highest-leverage decision in cereal agronomy: it is the only control anyone has over frost exposure, and it is exercised months in advance and never revisited. Second, there is an uncomfortable interaction with the conservation-agriculture material from L01 — heavy stubble loads raise frost risk, because standing residue insulates the soil and reduces the heat radiated back to the canopy overnight. The practice that conserves water and protects the surface also, on the wrong night, costs the grade. Put that to the room as a genuine trade-off with no clean answer; it is a good test of whether they have stopped looking for recipes.

Part 4

How Yield Is Actually Formed — and Lost

G × E × M, the compensation machinery, and water-use efficiency as the scoreboard that tells you whether any of it worked.

Target: ~17 min for this part. Preview: the organising framework (G × E × M), the WA evidence on how much each term actually contributes, then the compensation mechanism and its limits, then WUE as the diagnostic that closes the loop, and finally the integrated season-long program that ties Parts 3 and 4 together.

Genotype × Environment × Management

Genotype (G)

  • Phenology — maturity, photoperiod and vernalisation response
  • Yield potential and harvest index
  • Disease resistance
  • Grain quality class
  • Tolerances: frost, heat, drought, acidity, salinity

Environment (E)

  • Growing-season rainfall — amount, timing, reliability
  • Temperature extremes at flowering and fill
  • Soil water-holding capacity
  • Subsoil constraints — acidity, compaction, salinity
  • Disease and weed pressure carried by the site

Management (M)

  • Sowing date and seeding rate
  • Nutrition — rate, timing, placement
  • Weed, disease and pest control
  • Rotation and stubble
  • Harvest timing
The framework's whole point is the ×. The best variety depends on the environment (a quick variety for a late break, a slow one for early sowing). The best management depends on the variety and the season (a high-potential variety in a good season justifies high nitrogen; the same money is wasted on a drought).
Target: ~4 min. Introduce this as the organising principle that answers the question students actually ask: "so what should I do?" The honest answer is that there is no single best variety and no universal recipe — only the best combination of G, E and M for a given situation. That is not a cop-out, it is the discipline. Emphasise that the multiplication sign carries the meaning. If the terms were additive, you could optimise each separately and agronomy would be a lookup table. Because they interact, the value of any decision is conditional on the other two, and the conditioning is what makes the job skilled. Two worked examples to say aloud: (1) sowing date × variety — the same sowing date is early for a slow variety and late for a quick one, so date and variety must be chosen as a pair to land flowering in the safe window; (2) nitrogen × season — identical N rates return very differently depending on the spring, which is exactly why tactical N splitting exists and why crop models earn their keep. Flag forward: this framework is the reason crop models matter. A model can explore the full G × E × M factorial cheaply, over a hundred years of climate records, which no field program can.

How Cereals Compensate for Stress

  1. 1Thinned at establishment? The crop tillers harder and restores head number. Plants per m² is the most compensable component of all.
  2. 2Stressed during tillering? If conditions ease by flowering, the surviving heads can still set an adequate number of grains each.
  3. 3Short of grain number? A kind finish lets the crop put more weight into each grain it did set.
  4. 4The mechanism: because the components are set sequentially, a component reduced early can be partly offset by components set later — provided resources return. It is the same source–sink logic a crop model represents as stress factors.
This is why early stress is rarely fatal to a cereal, and why cereals recover from setbacks that would ruin a canola or pulse crop. It is the physiological basis of the risk argument in Part 1.
Target: ~3 min. Return explicitly to the yield-component diagram from before the break — this slide is that diagram read as a strategy. The crop is running a continuous negotiation with the season: it sets an ambitious target early, then revises it as information arrives. The clean way to express it: each yield component is an option the crop can exercise later, so a loss early is a reduction in one term of a product that still has three terms left to be determined. A canola crop has far fewer downstream options. Ask the room to reason through a case rather than telling them: "a crop establishes at 60 plants per square metre instead of 140 because the break was marginal. Walk me through what happens." Steer toward: more tillers per plant, head number partly restored, canopy closes later so more soil evaporation and more weed competition, maturity more variable, and grain possibly smaller. Tonnes largely defended; quality and timeliness quietly eroded. That distinction — compensation protects yield better than it protects quality — is the bridge to Part 5.

Where Compensation Runs Out

Compensation has hard limits, and both of them matter enormously in WA:

  • Losses at flowering are irreversible. Frost-sterilised florets and heat-damaged pollen destroy grain number — the largest component — at the one moment when only grain size remains to compensate with. There is not enough room left in the equation.
  • Terminal drought removes the later components' capacity to compensate. Compensation requires resources to return. If the profile is empty and the finish is hot, the crop cannot put extra weight into grain even though it "should".
  • So the timing of stress relative to development, not just its severity, determines whether the crop recovers.
  • And in a Mediterranean season these two limits coincide: flowering and grain fill both sit in the drying, warming end of the season.
Target: ~2 min. Do not let students leave Part 4 believing cereals are indestructible — the compensation story is seductive and needs this counterweight. The sharpest framing: compensation is a mechanism for converting early adversity into a smaller but viable crop. It offers nothing against late adversity, and WA's adversity is structurally late. That is precisely why the flowering-window decision — made at sowing, months in advance, and unchangeable thereafter — is the highest-leverage decision in cereal agronomy, and why so much breeding effort goes into phenology. Connect to the stress-timing table two slides back: the "very high" impact row was pollination, the one stage with no downstream compensation available.

Water-Use Efficiency: the Scoreboard

  • French–Schultz: water-limited potential yield rises with growing-season water use above an evaporation threshold of roughly 110 mm, at a slope representing transpiration efficiency.
  • Benchmark slopes: wheat ~20 kg/ha/mm (breeding target 25), barley and oats ~25, canola ~12.
  • Actual WA performance often falls well short — measured slopes of 4.9 kg/ha/mm at Morawa and 7.7 at Merredin. Some operations achieve 60–80% of potential; others reach 30%.
  • The gap is the diagnostic: a crop near the line was well managed for that season; a crop well below it has a correctable constraint.

The boundary is the water-limited potential; the vertical distance below it is the yield gap. Note the intercept — the first ~100 mm buys no grain at all.

Target: ~3 min. Students met French–Schultz in L01; today it stops being a formula and becomes a diagnostic instrument. The move to make explicit: the equation does not tell you what your yield will be, it tells you what it could have been given the water you actually got — and the residual is the part you are responsible for. Teach the figure in three moves rather than describing it. One — the intercept. Put a finger on where the line meets the x-axis, at about 100 mm, and ask what it means that the line does not start at the origin. A 150 mm season is not "half of a 300 mm season", it is 50 mm of useful water against 200 — a quarter. That non-linearity is why marginal seasons collapse so much faster than students expect. (The bullet says roughly 110 mm and the figure reads 100; say so plainly. Almost every point sits below the line, and the vertical drop is the yield gap for that paddock in that season. The opportunity for agronomy is largest exactly where conditions are best. Then run the diagnosis, which is the real content: a paddock well below the line has a constraint, and the candidates are finite — weeds competing for the same water, disease or nutrient deficiency limiting canopy function, subsoil acidity or compaction restricting root access to stored water (straight back to L03), or poorly matched phenology wasting the good part of the season. Each is testable. Close on the historical point, which reframes the whole unit: roughly 70% of historical yield gain in WA is attributed to management improvement rather than genetics — summer weed control, no-till and stubble retention keeping more rainfall available, earlier sowing aligning growth with the efficient part of the season, better nutrition and disease control keeping the canopy working. WUE is the metric that registers all of it. Finish by turning the scatter into a business question, which is the handover to L10: if you had one agronomist to deploy across a Merredin farm and a Kojonup farm, where does the hour earn more? The figure says Kojonup — the gap there is wider, so there is more to recover. The evidence backs it: the gap is measurably narrower below 250 mm of growing-season rainfall and wider above it. But do not let that stand as the whole answer, because rainfall zone raises yield potential and variable costs together. High-rainfall farming carries greater financial exposure per hectare, so the larger opportunity comes with the larger downside, and the low-input strategy in marginal country is a rational risk position rather than under-management. That tension — bigger gap, bigger exposure — is exactly what the gross-margin and risk material in L10 exists to resolve.

  • 1Pre-sowing & sowing. Choose variety and sowing date so flowering lands in the safe window. Set target density. Place seed and starter fertiliser accurately. Pre-emergent weed control.
  • 2Emergence – tillering (Z1x–Z2x). Protect establishment and tillering: timely post-emergent weed control, early nitrogen matched to the emerging outlook and stored moisture.
  • 3Stem elongation (Z30–Z39). Finish stage-critical herbicides before the growing point rises. Main nitrogen dressing to build grains-per-head in a promising season. Manage lodging risk.
  • 4Flowering (Z6x). The crop is committed. Protect the yield-forming canopy with fungicide if disease threatens. Frost and heat exposure are now fixed by decisions made at sowing.
  • 5Grain fill – harvest (Z7x–Z9x). Monitor late disease and insects — aphids during grain fill are a real threat. Harvest promptly at maturity to preserve quality.
Target: ~2 min. This is the synthesis of Parts 3 and 4 — the program an agronomist actually runs. Walk it once briskly; the detail is all on earlier slides. The point that must land is that this is not a recipe. It is a decision sequence that is continuously revised as the season reveals itself. At step 2 you commit modest nitrogen because you do not yet know what season you are in. By step 3 you know a great deal more — how much rain has fallen, how the profile looks, what the crop's biomass is telling you — and you invest accordingly. That is what "tactical agronomy under a variable climate" means in practice, and it is why nitrogen is split rather than applied all at seeding. Note the asymmetry in reversibility: steps 1–3 are decisions with options; from step 4 the crop is committed and you are only protecting what exists. Good agronomists spend their attention where the options are. Put numbers on the nitrogen sequence, because "split your N" is meaningless without them. The standard WA shape is a baseline 10–20 kg N/ha at seeding — enough to establish and start tillering, deliberately not enough to commit to a yield you cannot yet forecast — with the substantial decision deferred to Z30–Z31, once the break, the stored profile and the crop's own biomass have told you what season you are in. Map that onto the demand curve from Part 5: about 30% of uptake happens before first node and 40% between first node and flag leaf, so the deferred dressing is landing immediately ahead of peak demand rather than late. The framing worth giving them: splitting nitrogen is buying an option. Applying it all up-front is cheaper per pass but commits capital to a yield forecast made in April, when the forecast is worst. The extra pass is the premium you pay to decide in July instead. That is the same logic as the malt-versus-feed bet in Part 5 and the same logic L10 formalises as risk management — and it is why APSIM-type models earn their keep here specifically, at the point where a real decision is still open.

Part 5

Grain Quality and What It Is Worth

A grower does not sell tonnes; they sell tonnes of a specified grade. Quality is often the difference between a good year and an average one.

Target: ~25 min for this part. Preview: why grade matters at all, the measured parameters, then wheat (classification and protein, including the dilution effect and the nitrogen economy behind it), then barley (the malting premium, the protein window, screenings and brightness), and finally the practice checklist that turns all of it into a program. Tell students this section is where agronomy meets the market and that it is the direct handoff to L10 (farm financial analysis) and L13 (logistics).

Why Grain Quality Determines Price

Grain is bought against strict published specifications, not by appearance or goodwill:

  • Grain meeting a higher-value classification earns a substantial premium; grain failing a specification is downgraded — sometimes from milling wheat straight to feed — with a large price penalty.
  • Standards are public and administered through Grain Trade Australia and the receival networks (CBH in WA, GrainCorp, Viterra).
  • Grade is assessed at the weighbridge, on the day, on a sample. The decision is fast, mechanical and final.
  • Because quality is shaped by variety, nutrition and the season, and can be won or lost in the final weeks, managing for quality runs through variety choice, nitrogen strategy and harvest timing — not just the last fortnight.
Target: ~2 min. Reframe the grower's product deliberately: not "wheat" but "a delivered load that satisfies a specification". Students who have spent the last ninety minutes optimising tonnes need to feel the shift. The number that makes it concrete: a downgrade from a milling grade to feed can cost on the order of $50–80 per tonne on prices in the $300s. On a 3 t/ha crop over 1000 ha that is a six-figure difference produced by a specification, not by a yield. Emphasise the finality — there is no appeal to how good the season was or how well the crop was managed. The sample either meets the spec or it does not. That asymmetry is why quality risk is managed defensively, months in advance, and it is exactly the mindset the gross-margin and risk material in L10 formalises. Ground the whole part in the export fact from Part 1: about 90% of WA grain leaves the country. The specification is not a local convention a grower might argue with — it is the interface to a buyer in Japan, Indonesia or the Middle East who is purchasing a functional input for a factory, sight unseen, against a written standard. That is why the standards are administered centrally through Grain Trade Australia and the receival networks rather than negotiated paddock by paddock, and why WA has specialised into narrow classes such as the noodle wheats: the market is specific, distant, and unforgiving. The forward connection to make explicitly: this is the point where L06 hands over to L13 (logistics) — segregation at the bin, and the fact that once your load is tipped into a stack it takes that stack's identity — and to L14 (global markets), which explains who is buying and why the classes exist at all.

What Actually Gets Measured

Chemical & functional

  • Protein content — central for wheat; determines suitability for bread, noodles or feed. Directly priced.
  • Falling number — sprouting damage from untimely rain. Low values mean high alpha-amylase and degraded dough; critical for milling and noodle wheats.
  • Moisture — must be below threshold (max 12.5%) for safe storage.
  • Germination and variety purity — malting barley only, and non-negotiable.

Physical

  • Test weight (hectolitre weight, kg/hL) — soundness and packing density.
  • Screenings — the fraction passing a slotted sieve. The direct measure of a pinched, drought-affected finish.
  • Grain colour / brightness — weather staining; a real issue near the coast.
  • Hardness, and for barley skinning (husk loss, which causes uneven malting).
Falling number, explained: starch heated in water makes a thick paste, so the plunger takes many seconds to fall. Sprouted grain has broken that starch down to sugars, the paste is watery, and the plunger drops quickly. A low falling number literally means the starch has already been digested.
Target: ~2 min. Do not just list — group them by what they are actually detecting, which is what makes them memorable. Protein detects the nitrogen economy of the crop. Screenings and test weight detect the finish — they are the fingerprint of terminal drought, and everything in Part 3 about truncated grain fill shows up here. Falling number detects untimely rain after maturity. Brightness detects humidity and weathering. Each parameter is a sensor pointed at a different part of the season. Spend the extra thirty seconds on the falling number mechanism — students remember it once they understand it is a viscosity test and that a low number means the grain has begun digesting itself. It is also a genuinely cruel parameter agronomically: a crop can be perfect all season and be downgraded by a single November rain event on ripe standing grain, which is why prompt harvest is a quality decision.

Protein and the Dilution Effect

  • Protein concentration is a ratio: nitrogen accumulated in the grain, divided by the mass of grain produced.
  • So a big yield can dilute protein below the milling threshold even when total nitrogen uptake is unchanged. Moisture during grain fill drives starch accumulation, which is the denominator.
  • The photographs show it directly: essentially identical yield, 1.4 percentage points of protein apart — the thicker canopy diluted the same nitrogen into the same tonnage of grain differently.
  • The agronomist's problem: hit a yield target and a protein target simultaneously, under a season you cannot see yet.
Target: ~3 min. This is the subtlest idea in Part 5 and worth the time. Start by killing the intuition students arrive with: "more nitrogen means more protein" is only true holding yield constant, and yield is never constant. Protein is a ratio, and nitrogen moves both the numerator and (via yield) the denominator. Then use the photographs, which are the cleanest illustration you will get: two canopies, 6.18 versus 6.20 t/ha — a rounding error in yield — and 12.0% versus 10.6% protein. On the wheat classification table, that difference is the difference between clearing an AH minimum and not. The cruel corollary for growers: a good season can cost you your grade. A generous spring lifts yield, dilutes protein, and drops a crop from a premium milling grade into a lower one — more tonnes, less money per tonne, and sometimes less money in total. This is why "grow for yield" and "grow for quality" are genuinely different strategies rather than the same strategy described twice. Ask the room: "your crop is looking exceptional in early September. Does that make you more or less likely to topdress nitrogen?" (More — both because the yield potential justifies it and because you now need more nitrogen just to hold protein.) Widen it into the canopy-management trade-off, because the photographs are really about canopy density and it is worth naming both sides. A thicker canopy generally yields more and competes better with weeds through shading — but it also carries more disease risk and greater exposure to frost damage, and, as the photographs show, it dilutes protein. So the question is never "is a thick canopy good?" but "is the extra yield worth the protein I give up, the disease and frost risk I take on, and the cost of the product and the pass?" That is a gross-margin question, and it is exactly the shape of the decisions in L10.

The Nitrogen Economy Behind the Protein

  • Cereal N uptake has two phases: accumulation that drives growth and yield potential, then remobilisation to the grain that sets protein.
  • Phased across the season: ~30% before first node, 40% first node → flag leaf, 20% flag leaf → flowering, 10% after.
  • By flowering, 80–90% of crop N has accumulated; peak demand is at stem elongation. At maturity ~75% of shoot N has been remobilised into the grain (60–80%).
  • Under high N rates the proportion remobilised falls; late uptake during grain fill keeps leaves green, favours starch, and can lower protein.
  • The tactic: N at flag leaf emergence is too late to build yield but can still lift protein. APSIM estimates the rate that lands protein in a target band, not just maximum tonnes.

N uptake against growth stage: most of the crop's nitrogen is taken up in the construction phase, Z30–Z61. (Northern-hemisphere calendar; read the growth stages, not the months.)

Target: ~2 min. This slide explains why the topdressing windows in Part 3 are where they are — the demand curve is the reason, not convention. Warn about the figure honestly: the x-axis months are northern hemisphere. Tell students to read the GS labels and ignore the calendar. The shape is what transfers. Two implications worth stating plainly. First, nitrogen applied after flowering is mostly too late to build yield — only about 10% of uptake happens then — but it can still lift protein, which is why a late flag-leaf-emergence application is a recognised protein tactic rather than a yield tactic. Second, the remobilisation figure means that most of the protein in the grain was in the leaves first. Anything that kills the canopy prematurely — disease, drought, frost — interrupts a transfer that was already scheduled. The counter-intuitive last bullet is worth dwelling on: at high N rates a crop stays green, keeps taking up soil N during fill, keeps photosynthesising and lays down starch — and the protein percentage can end up lower than expected. Yet another face of the dilution effect.

Barley: the Malt-versus-Feed Decision

Malt (Malt 1 / Malt 2)

  • Accredited varieties only — 95% varietal purity.
  • Protein min 9–9.5%, max ~12.5–12.8%.
  • Screenings (2.5 mm) Malt 1 ≤ 20%, Malt 2 ≤ 35%; premium malt grades target <7% through 2.2 mm.
  • Test weight ≥ 64 kg/hL. Moisture ≤ 12.5%.
  • Must germinate uniformly and vigorously — sprouting or weathering disqualifies.
  • Premium price.

Feed

  • Any variety.
  • No protein limits.
  • No screenings limit.
  • Test weight ≥ 56 kg/hL. Moisture ≤ 12.5%.
  • Lower price — but a genuine, reliable floor.
Whether to grow for malt depends on four things: the premium on offer; the relative yield of malting versus feed varieties; the agronomic and disease constraints of the malting varieties; and the likelihood that the grain will actually make the grade.
Target: ~3 min. The structural insight: barley is the only crop in this unit where the grower makes a market decision at sowing and then spends the season trying to validate it, with a known and acceptable fallback if they fail. Work the four factors in the box as a genuine decision problem. A large premium justifies accepting a malting variety's yield penalty and tighter agronomy; a small premium does not. And the fourth factor — the probability of actually making grade — is a seasonal probability the grower cannot control, which makes this an expected-value calculation, not a plan. That framing is exactly what L10's risk-management material formalises, so signpost it. Note that the feed floor is what makes the bet reasonable: unlike a canola crop that fails, barley that misses malt is still a saleable commodity at a known price. That is the risk argument from Part 1 reappearing in a market rather than an agronomic form. Two asides worth thirty seconds each if the room is with you. Where the premium goes: beer is the number one quality focus for the WA malting market, but the same grain also supplies shochu, whisky and other distilled spirits — which is part of why the specification is written for a processor's convenience rather than a nutritionist's. Where the crop came from: domesticated barley (Hordeum vulgare) descends from wild barley (H. spontaneum), one of some 31 Hordeum species — and two of the others, H. leporinum and H. glaucum, are the barley grasses students will meet as weeds. Selective control inside a barley crop is correspondingly awkward, which is a neat illustration of why crop and weed taxonomy is an agronomic problem and not a botanical curiosity. Push that one step further, because it closes a loop students enjoy: Hordeum glaucum is a green-bridge host for net blotch, which is stubble-borne and one of the diseases that drives screenings up. So the weedy relative is not merely hard to spray out — it is maintaining the inoculum that costs you the malting grade. Weed control, disease control and quality are one problem, not three. Two more things worth having ready. First, the accreditation is not a single number: malt grade turns on seven criteria — germination, variety purity, hectolitre weight, protein, screenings, colour and skinning — and all of them must pass. Students who have been optimising protein need to hear that a load can be perfect on protein and still fail on skinning. Seven independent gates is a low joint probability, which is the real reason the malt premium exists. Second, the yield penalty is real and quantifiable in the variety tables: feed varieties such as Leebrook generally out-yield the malt-accredited varieties. So the malt decision is not "free upside" — a grower gives up tonnes at sowing for a chance at a premium they may not collect. Ask the room to state the break-even in words: how large must the premium be, and how confident must you be of making grade, to justify the yield you are surrendering? That is an expected-value calculation and it is exactly the L10 material.

The Malting Protein Window: Aim at a Band, Not a Ceiling

<9.5%Too low. Weak yeast fermentation, reduced beer head duration and stability, poor clinical performance in the brewery.
9.5–12.5%The window. The target for accredited malting barley — and the reason barley nitrogen management is fundamentally different from wheat's.
>12.5%Too high. Less alcohol per tonne, longer brewery filtration times, beer haze risk, shorter shelf life.
The trap that catches WA growers: a legume-rich rotation raises background soil nitrogen. In a dry, low-yielding season there is not enough grain to dilute it, and protein overshoots 12.5% — losing the malting premium because the rotation was good. Hence the standard advice: put barley after a non-legume (canola or wheat) when chasing malt.
Target: ~3 min. Make the contrast with wheat explicit and let it land, because it inverts everything students just learned: in wheat, protein is a minimum to be cleared and more is generally better. In malting barley it is a band, and overshooting is as costly as undershooting. Same nutrient, same crop family, opposite objective function. Explain the two failure modes physically, not just as rules — the brewer's problems are real chemistry. High protein means proportionally less starch, so less fermentable extract per tonne, plus haze-forming proteins and slower filtration. Low protein means too little free amino nitrogen for the yeast and insufficient protein to stabilise the head. Then the rotation trap, which is the best example in the unit of an interaction with a counter-intuitive sign: the lupin phase that improved the system's nitrogen, water use and disease status is the same phase that costs the barley crop its premium in a dry year. Ask students to reconcile that with the pro-legume argument from Part 1 — the answer is sequencing, not avoidance: legume, then wheat to draw the nitrogen down, then barley. Give the trap its timescale, because that is what makes it a rotation problem rather than a fertiliser one: legume nitrogen persists in a comparatively stable form for up to about two years, while synthetic N is far more readily leached from these sandy profiles. So a grower cannot solve an overshoot by simply not applying N — the nitrogen is already in the system, mineralising on the season's terms rather than theirs. The only real controls are how many years of separation you leave and what you grow in between. Then close the loop with the dilution effect two slides back, because together they explain the cruellest version of the trap: overshoot is a dry-season failure. In a good year the yield dilutes the background N and protein lands in the window; in a dry year the same soil nitrogen concentrates into a small crop and pushes past 12.5%. So the grower loses the premium in precisely the year they most needed it, and the mechanism was set in motion two rotations earlier. Ask the room what that implies for how far ahead a malting-barley decision is really made — the answer is years, not seasons, which is the strongest argument in the lecture for planning rotations as sequences rather than one paddock at a time.

Screenings: the Signature of a Hard Finish

  • Screenings measure grain plumpness — the fraction passing a 2.0–2.2 mm sieve. Target <10%; <7% for Malt 1.
  • Plumpness sets how much grain is maltable and how evenly it takes up water. Going 10% → 20% screenings costs about 3% less maltable grain.
  • Driven primarily by moisture stress during grain fill — the "soft finish" is the biggest lever, and it is the season's to give. Also disease (powdery mildew, net blotch, scald), frost at milk–soft dough (up to 70%), and early ryegrass competition.
  • The N × water interaction: nitrogen is the gas pedal, water is the fuel tank. N without spring moisture builds a canopy that cannot be filled — and increases screenings.
Target: ~2 min. Screenings is the parameter that connects Part 5 back most directly to Part 3 — it is the delivered, priced measurement of the truncated grain fill we described. The gas-pedal-and-fuel-tank metaphor is from the industry material and it works well, so use it: nitrogen is the accelerator that sets how much canopy and how many grain sites the crop commits to; water is the fuel that has to fill them. Flooring the accelerator with an empty tank does not go faster — it strands the crop with more grain sites than it can fill, and every one of those under-filled sites is a screening. This is why the same nitrogen rate that is correct in a wet spring is damaging in a dry one, and it is measurable: in dry springs high-N treatments show clearly increased screenings at some WA sites, while in average springs the effect largely vanishes. On disease: fungicide trials show the effect on screenings is often larger and more reliable than the effect on yield. That is worth saying explicitly, because a fungicide decision evaluated only on expected yield response will be under-valued in malting barley. Note also the counter-intuitive negatives from the WA data: seed rate and row spacing turn out to be much weaker drivers of screenings than water stress, disease and early weed competition. Start on the photograph, because it makes an abstract percentage physical. Those are slotted grading sieves — note the apertures are slots, not round holes, so what is being measured is grain width, not length or volume. A grain that is long but pinched still falls through. Point out that the same sample of grain grades differently on a 2.0 and a 2.2 mm slot, which is exactly why the standard names the slot width. The screenings figure is not a judgement about the crop; it is a mechanical sort, done in about a minute, on a sample taken at the weighbridge. Give them the size arithmetic so the threshold means something: individual grain weight in a decent WA finish runs about 30–40 mg. Terminal drought moves grain down that range and across the slot. So the difference between making Malt 1 at <7% screenings and missing it is a few milligrams per grain — a quantity no one can see in a paddock, which is why growers cannot reliably predict their own grade before delivery. Then make the N × water interaction site-specific, because "it depends on the season" is unsatisfying without evidence. The WA trial work shows genuinely divergent site behaviour: under high N in a dry spring, screenings rose clearly at some sites (Kalingari) while others (Beverley) stayed below the critical threshold. Same nitrogen decision, opposite quality outcome, decided by the site's capacity to carry moisture into spring. That is precisely why item 2 on the malting checklist — choose paddocks that hold moisture into spring — is a quality decision, and it is a concrete instance of the G × E × M interaction term dominating the main effects, exactly as the variance table in Part 4 predicted. Row spacing is worth a sentence as a regional contrast even though it is a weak lever: coastal WA tends to 22 cm, the drier eastern belt 30 cm, the wider rows being a moisture-conservation choice. Use it to reinforce the ranking — growers adjust the weak levers because they are the ones they control, while the strong lever (spring moisture) is the season's to give.

Grain Brightness: the One You Cannot Manage Your Way Out Of

  • Yellowing — discolouration from light showers or high afternoon humidity. No fungi involved. Measured as brightness; target >59 L.
  • Greying — discolouration from heavy showers, associated with moulds and fungi. Target <5% mould-affected grains per 400.
  • Little direct effect on malting, but the grain is less suited to the pale-beer market, and mould can induce dormancy and lengthen storage.
  • Driven by variety, sowing date, 3 pm relative humidity, and rain during maturity and harvest.
  • Being near the coast is a structural disadvantage. Best agronomy delivers only minor improvement — the real solution is a brighter variety.
Target: ~2 min. Include this precisely because it is the counter-example to the empowering message of the rest of the lecture. Almost everything today has been "here is a lever the grower can pull". Brightness is largely E and G, with very little M — it is set by afternoon humidity and rain at maturity, which is to say by where the farm is. South-coast growers carry this risk permanently and best-practice agronomy shifts it only marginally. The honest conclusion — choose a brighter variety — is a genuinely useful lesson about the structure of agronomic problems: when M is weak, the answer is G, and that is what breeding programs are for. It also demonstrates that "manage it better" is not always the right recommendation, which is a piece of professional judgement worth modelling explicitly for third-years.

Growing Malting Barley: the Practice Checklist

  • 1Rotation. Follow a non-legume — canola or wheat. A legume phase leaves background nitrogen that overshoots the protein ceiling in a dry year.
  • 2Soil type. Paddocks that hold moisture into spring. The most critical site decision for malting barley: plumpness is made or lost in the finish.
  • 3Time of sowing. As early as the variety safely allows — it lengthens grain fill and moves it off the hot end of the season.
  • 4Nitrogen. Match rate to the yield and the protein target. Site and rate are primary; variety and split timing secondary.
  • 5Seeding rate. Target 120–150 plants/m². Only really a lever with late sowing.
  • 6Disease. Seed dressing, then monitor foliar disease — net type and spot type net blotch, scald, powdery mildew. The effect on screenings is often larger and more reliable than the effect on yield.
  • 7Aphids. Control feeding during grain fill; that is the critical window.
  • 8Weeds. Take them out early, within an integrated weed management program.
Choose the variety, then choose the management — accredited malting varieties in WA include Baudin, Buloke, Bass, Flinders, Maximus CL and Spartacus, and the program is built around the one you picked. APSIM can estimate the N rate that lands protein in the 10–11% band.

Walk it briskly — every item has already been argued for somewhere in the lecture, so this is consolidation, not new content. The framing sentence is the one in the box: choose the variety, then choose the management. Students want a universal recipe and this is the closest the unit comes to refusing them one. The management program is conditional on the genotype, which is the G × M interaction arriving as a practical instruction. Two items deserve a beat each. Item 1 is the counter-intuitive one — the rotation advice for malting barley runs against the pro-legume argument from Part 1, and the resolution is sequencing rather than avoidance: legume, then wheat to draw the nitrogen down, then barley. Item 6 is the one that changes a spending decision: if a fungicide is evaluated only on expected yield response it will be systematically under-valued in malting barley, because the quality response is the bigger part of the return. Point that out — an honest checklist that admits its own gap is more useful than a complete-looking one. On item 2, tie it to the Kalingari-versus-Beverley evidence from the screenings slide: the paddock that holds moisture into spring is the paddock where a given N rate does not cost you plumpness. How Agronomy, Soils and Climate Shape Yield and Quality

Climate sets the envelope

  • Growing-season rainfall and its reliability cap yield through water supply.
  • Frost at flowering and heat at fill impose hard penalties on grain number and size.
  • The terminal drought that ends most WA seasons is the dominant influence on plumpness and screenings.

Soils modulate it

  • Water-holding capacity determines how much rainfall is stored for the dry finish.
  • Subsoil constraints — acidity, compaction, salinity — restrict root access to that stored water.
  • Nutrient status governs canopy function and grain protein.

Agronomy is the lever

  • Variety and sowing date to land flowering in the safe window.
  • Nutrition to build yield and hit a protein target.
  • Weed, disease and pest control to protect the canopy and the water it uses.
  • Timely harvest to preserve quality.
Target: ~3 min. This answers the last learning outcome directly — say so. The structure to emphasise is the nesting: climate sets an envelope, soil determines how much of that envelope is actually accessible to roots, and agronomy operates inside what is left. A grower cannot expand the envelope, can slowly improve the soil's access to it over years (liming, compaction management — the L03 material), and can adjust agronomy every season. Reconcile this with the variance table from Part 4: E dominated the variance, which is exactly what "climate sets the envelope" means quantitatively. But the interactions were often the largest single term, which is exactly what "agronomy is the lever inside the envelope" means. The two slides are the same claim in different languages. Give the three columns their WA numbers so the nesting is not just a diagram. Climate: about 100–110 mm of growing-season rainfall never reaches the crop at all, lost to soil evaporation — that is the envelope shrinking before agronomy gets a turn. Soil: the water-limited potential is roughly 20 kg/ha/mm for wheat and 25 for barley, but measured WA performance has run as low as 4.9 kg/ha/mm at Morawa and 7.7 at Merredin — and the gap between those numbers is very largely a story about roots reaching stored water, which is the L03 subsoil material. Agronomy: roughly 70% of historical yield gain in WA is attributed to management rather than genetics. Those three figures, read together, are the whole lecture in one line: the envelope is small, much of it is inaccessible, and almost all the improvement we have ever made came from the third column. Then take it to the business, which is where L10 picks up: rainfall zone lifts yield potential and variable costs together, so a high-rainfall farm has more upside and more exposure per hectare, while the low-input strategy in marginal country is a deliberate risk position rather than under-management. Ask the room which farm can afford to be wrong about nitrogen. Finish by naming the through-line for the unit: every lecture from here is either widening access to the envelope (L03 soils, L11 precision ag), protecting what is inside it (L07–L09 agronomy, weeds and disease), or pricing what comes out of it (L10, L13, L14).

Bringing it together

  • Development is the clockEvery input is timed by growth stage, not date, because the crop's readiness — not the calendar — determines the response. Learn to stage a paddock and you can time anything.
  • Compensation is the safety netSequentially-set yield components let cereals absorb early stress. That machinery is the physiological reason cereals are lower-risk than break crops — and it fails exactly at flowering and in a terminal drought.
  • Quality is the price tagYield and quality are the joint outcome of a genotype expressing itself through a season under a grower's management. Skilled agronomy converts scarce water into the greatest value of grain that meets a specification.
