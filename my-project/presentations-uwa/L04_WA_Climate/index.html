What Is Agrometeorology? Weather vs Climate

The physical science of weather and climate as it affects agriculture — the toolkit for turning atmospheric and soil measurements into agronomic decisions:

  • Weather — the day-to-day conditions a grower reacts to (today's forecast, this week's frost risk).
  • Climate — the long-term statistical distribution of weather that shapes strategy (variety choice, rotation, capital investment).
  • In a rainfed system: rainfall sets the ceiling on yield, temperature governs the pace of development and frost/heat risk, and their interplay defines the growing season.
This lecture builds the vocabulary and equations every later lecture assumes you already have
Target: ~3 min. Land the weather/climate distinction early and cleanly — it's easy to blur and the rest of today depends on students keeping them separate. Preview the whole session's structure on this slide since it's the natural place to set expectations: Part 1 (today's longest) builds the physical toolkit, Part 2 applies it to WA's Mediterranean growing season, Part 3 (after a break) covers why rainfall varies so much year to year, and Part 4 covers the long-term drying trend. Flag explicitly that this is deliberately a "why" and "how to calculate" lecture rather than a "what to do" lecture like L03 — today hands students the physical mechanisms and tools; later lectures hand them the resulting agronomic decisions.

Irradiance, Day Length and Temperature

Solar input

  • Irradiance — solar radiation reaching the crop.
  • PAR (photosynthetically active radiation) — the ~400–700 nm fraction that actually drives photosynthesis.
  • Both follow daily and seasonal patterns governed by day length (photoperiod) and cloud cover.

The winter trade-off

  • Temperature follows a predictable seasonal cycle in a Mediterranean climate — cool in the growing season, hot in summer (the chart later this Part shows this directly).
  • WA's winter growing season has short days and lower light than summer — crops are bred and managed around this genuine light/temperature trade-off, not just around water.
Target: ~3 min. Keep this brisk — it's the "energy" leg of agrometeorology, rounding out the picture before the much larger "water" leg that follows. The winter trade-off point is worth landing clearly: WA's growing season exists in winter because that's when water is available (next slides), not because light is optimal then — light is actually working against the crop in winter (short days, lower sun angle), which is part of why variety phenology and sowing timing (L03) matter so much for capturing the best available light within the water-defined window. PAR will resurface implicitly whenever canopy closure or light interception is discussed in later crop-specific lectures.

Solar Radiation: Winter vs Summer

Winter growing season

  • Solar radiation: 8–10 MJ/m²/day (June–August)
  • Unlikely to limit crop growth, though crop and pasture leaf areas remain low
  • Second most important climatic element after rainfall — determines temperature and evaporation regimes

Summer conditions

  • Solar energy: up to 30 MJ/m²/day
  • Driven by high sun elevation, clear atmosphere, cloudless skies
  • Among the greatest radiation loads at Earth's surface — explains productivity of irrigated summer crops in Mediterranean climates
Target: ~3 min. Quantify the winter/summer radiation difference explicitly — the threefold increase from 8-10 to 30 MJ/m²/day is why summer crops under irrigation can be so productive despite the heat stress, since they have both abundant light and controlled water. The "unlikely to limit growth" qualifier for winter radiation is important: winter crops are water-limited and cold-limited far more often than they are light-limited, even though the light regime is objectively lower than summer. This connects forward to the evaporation discussion (next slides) — summer's extreme radiation load drives the high evaporative demand that empties the soil water tank during terminal drought.

In WA the "lift and cool" step is delivered mainly by cold fronts, and less often by cut-off lows and northwest cloudbands (Part 3).

Target: ~4 min. Build this cycle on the chalkboard alongside the slide. The physics in one sentence: warm air can hold more water vapour than cool air, so cooling an air mass reduces its capacity to hold vapour, forcing condensation once the dew point is reached — that's the whole mechanism behind every rain event WA gets. Condensation itself needs tiny particles to form around (condensation nuclei — dust, sea salt, pollution), which is why even saturated air sometimes needs a nucleation source before droplets actually form. The shaded "lift and cool" step is highlighted deliberately: it's the step WA's specific weather systems (cold fronts, cut-off lows, cloudbands) actually provide, and Part 3's four climate drivers are really just asking "how often does the lift-and-cool step happen, and how much moisture is available when it does" — this diagram is the mechanism underneath that entire later discussion.

Beyond Rain Formation: Where the Water Goes Next

  • Once rain lands, it splits three ways: infiltration into the soil (feeding the soil water "tank" of the next few slides), surface runoff across the paddock, or deep drainage past the root zone toward groundwater.
  • Baseflow — groundwater slowly discharging back to streams — is why some rivers keep flowing long after the last rain.
  • The ice/snow/mountain elements of this classic diagram aren't WA-relevant — no snowmelt feeds the grainbelt — but the infiltration → soil storage → drainage/runoff chain is exactly what the soil water balance equation (next slide) is accounting for.
Target: ~3 min. Use this figure to extend the previous slide's atmospheric story into the ground: the previous mermaid diagram ended at "precipitation falls" — this diagram picks up exactly there and asks "then what?" Walk the three-way split explicitly, since it's the direct physical basis for every term in the soil water balance equation on the next slide: infiltration is the "input" term becoming stored soil water; deep drainage and surface runoff are two of the three "output" terms (the third, evapotranspiration, isn't pictured here since it returns water to the atmosphere rather than moving it through the landscape). Flag the non-WA elements openly rather than silently ignoring them — students should be able to critically read a generic diagram and identify which parts transfer to a WA dryland context and which don't; this is a useful transferable skill, not just a WA fact. Baseflow is worth a one-line real-world anchor: it's why a stream in the WA wheatbelt can still show a trickle weeks after the last rain, fed by groundwater discharge rather than direct runoff.

The Soil Water Balance: The Accounting Framework

ΔStorage = (Rainfall + Stored soil water) − (Evapotranspiration + Drainage + Runoff)

  • Inputs — rainfall (and irrigation where used) plus whatever moisture is already stored in the profile.
  • Outputs — evapotranspiration (evaporation + transpiration), drainage below the root zone, and surface runoff.
  • What's left after outputs is what the crop actually has available to grow on.
L01's French-Schultz equation is a simplified version of this: rainfall minus one lumped evaporative-loss term
Target: ~4 min. This is the master accounting framework the rest of Part 1 fills in piece by piece — every following slide (rain formation supplies the input term, PAW/SMD describe the storage term, frost relates to how the stored-energy side of the balance behaves) is really elaborating one part of this one equation. Draw the explicit connection to L01: French-Schultz (Yield = (GSR − 110 mm) × WUE) folds evapotranspiration, drainage and runoff into a single empirical "110 mm non-productive loss" constant — today's equation is the same idea, just unpacked into its real physical components rather than one lumped number. This is also the same accounting framework a soil-moisture probe or a model like APSIM/Yield Prophet runs in the background, just updated daily instead of summarised for a season.

Soil Water-Holding Capacity: Three Reference Points

Three reference points define the water any soil can offer a crop — the next slide fills the tank in.

Target: ~4 min. Introduce the three lines as a fixed property of a given soil (governed by texture — recall L03's texture triangle: a clay holds far more water at field capacity than a sand, but also holds more tightly near wilting point). Walk the logic of each boundary concretely: above field capacity, water simply drains away under gravity faster than roots can use it (so "topping up" past field capacity is wasted rain from the crop's perspective); below wilting point, the soil still physically contains some water, but it's held so tightly the crop's roots can no longer pull it out — the plant wilts and can't recover without more water. This tank sits directly inside the soil water balance equation from the previous slide: it's the physical container the "stored soil water" term describes. The next slide fills this same tank in to show what's actually usable.

Plant-Available Water & Soil-Moisture Deficit

PAW is the tank's capacity (fixed by soil texture); SMD is how empty it is right now (changes daily).

Target: ~5 min. Present these as a matched pair, not two separate topics — that's the whole point of the "two views of the same tank" framing. Walk both equations with the illustrative numbers on the diagram: PAW = field capacity − wilting point (here, 60% − 20% = 40 percentage points of profile depth — the maximum water this soil could ever offer a crop). SMD = field capacity − current soil moisture (here, 60% − 40% = 20 percentage points — how much rain is needed to fully refill the profile back to field capacity right now). Land the key distinction hard: PAW is a fixed soil property (bigger for clay, smaller for sand — recall L03's texture triangle), while SMD is a time-varying state that changes daily with every rainfall and evapotranspiration event, which is exactly why SMD is the number a soil- moisture probe or a model like Yield Prophet reports in-season, while PAW is closer to a one-off soil-characterisation number. A common student error is treating SMD as if it were a soil property too — stress it isn't, it's a snapshot in time.

Real weekly data from a UWA PLNT2201 radiation-lab wheat simulation (sown 1 June, root-zone PAW capacity 28 mm) — the same "tank" from the previous slide, now with real numbers.

Target: ~4 min. This is the previous slide's schematic tank made real — say that connection explicitly, since it's the whole point of showing this chart here rather than later. Frost Risk: Why Moisture Matters as Much as Temperature

Radiation frost forms on clear, calm nights when the ground loses heat to space faster than it's replaced — three ingredients:

  • Clear skies — no cloud blanket to reflect outgoing heat back down.
  • Still air — no wind to mix warmer air down to the surface.
  • Low humidity — dry air is a poor absorber of outgoing radiation (water vapour is itself a greenhouse gas), so heat escapes to space faster.
Moist air buffers against frost twice over: it traps outgoing heat, and condensing water releases latent heat that slows further cooling
Target: ~4 min. Land the counterintuitive point explicitly, since it's the whole reason this slide exists: it is not simply "cold equals frost." A cold but cloudy, humid, windy night often produces less frost than a cold, clear, dry, calm night at the same average temperature — moisture is doing as much work as raw temperature. This is also the physical basis for a real practical frost-mitigation technique worth mentioning: overhead irrigation before a frost night works because water releasing latent heat as it cools and partially freezes buffers the crop against the full temperature drop. WA specifics: frost risk is worst in low-lying valleys (cold, dense air drains downhill and pools overnight) and on bare, dry, sandy soils, which lose daytime heat faster and don't re-radiate stored warmth overnight the way a moist, heavy soil does — soil moisture is protective at the ground level exactly the same way atmospheric moisture is protective above it. Tie forward explicitly to Part 3: the exact synoptic setup that produces a frost night — a cold front passing, followed by a clear, dry, calm high- pressure cell — is the same subtropical-ridge-and-cold-front system covered later today.

Target: ~5 min. Walk the worked example on the board exactly as tabulated: each day's average temperature above base is that day's GDD contribution, and days simply accumulate — after three days this illustrative crop has banked 35°C-days of thermal time. Explain the payoff: over a full season, thousands of accumulated degree-days predict when a variety reaches key growth stages (emergence, tillering, flowering, maturity) far more reliably than counting calendar days, because development tracks accumulated heat, not the calendar — this is exactly how models like APSIM and Yield Prophet (already referenced in earlier lectures) simulate phenology without needing a fixed sowing-to-flowering day count. Different base temperatures are used for different crops and purposes in the wider literature; the 0°C base here is a simple, commonly used convention for illustration, not a single universal constant. Critical Periods: When Timing Matters Most

Not every day of the season matters equally — specific developmental windows have disproportionate effects on yield:

  • Timed using GDD/thermal time rather than the calendar, since development tracks accumulated heat, not the date.
  • Flowering is the single most sensitive window — this is where frost (previous slides) and heat (Part 2) do the most damage.
  • The same GDD-based timing logic governs input decisions too — e.g. 2,4-D herbicide must wait until after a defined growth stage to avoid crop distortion.
Target: ~3 min. This is a short bridging slide, not a new major topic — its job is to tie together everything just covered (GDD quantifies timing, frost/moisture determines risk) and hand off cleanly to Part 2, where the growing season's own critical events (the break, terminal drought) are defined. Land the general principle once, clearly: agronomic risk is not evenly spread across the season, it concentrates at specific developmental windows, and thermal time rather than the calendar is how those windows are predicted and managed. This is deliberately the last slide of Part 1 — everything from here on in today's lecture uses this toolkit rather than building more of it.

The Mediterranean Pattern: Winter Rain, Summer Drought

  • Delivered by cold fronts in the westerly wind belt, which migrates north over the continent in winter; summer is hot and dry under the subtropical ridge (Part 3).
  • A strong spatial gradient runs across the grainbelt — from over 1000 mm on the far south-west coast down to under 275 mm at the eastern margin.
  • The 275, 450 and 800 mm isohyets (green lines) mark the 1910–1999 baseline — DPIRD's 2000–2024 zoning shows those same rainfall totals now occurring further west and south than they used to.

Source: DPIRD, "South-west WA average annual rainfall zones 2000–2024 compared to 1910–1999."

Target: ~4 min. Recall the seasonal chart from Part 1 for the overlap-in-time story (rain and cool conditions coinciding May-October); this map adds the overlap-in-space story — the gradient across the grainbelt Part 1's single-location chart couldn't show. Walk the isohyets literally: they're contour lines of equal rainfall, exactly like elevation contours but for millimetres of rain per year. The map's own comparison — 2000-2024 against the 1910-1999 baseline — is worth pausing on explicitly since it's not just a gradient snapshot, it's already showing Part 4's drying trend spatially: the same isohyet that used to sit further east/north now sits further west/south, meaning the rainfall zone boundaries have themselves shifted toward the coast over the past century. This gradient is why agronomic recommendations are always zone-specific — a Colwell P critical value or a lime rate calibrated for a 500 mm southern-margin paddock (L03) genuinely doesn't transfer to a 300 mm eastern paddock, and this map is the reason why.

Defining the Growing Season: Break, Length, Terminal Drought

  • 1 The break — first substantial autumn rains (~25 mm over a few days) that wet soil enough to sow; timing ranges April–July.
  • 2 The growing season — the period when rainfall exceeds evaporative demand, running through winter and early spring.
  • 3 Terminal drought — when spring rain stops and rising temperature/evaporation exhaust soil moisture, forcing grain fill on stored water.
Target: ~4 min. These three events bracket every timing decision in the unit, and now students have the tools from Part 1 to understand each mechanistically: the break is the first time the soil water balance (P1-4) turns decisively positive after summer's deficit; the growing season is the period when that balance stays positive; terminal drought is when it turns negative again as evapotranspiration outpaces rainfall, drawing the soil tank down from field capacity toward wilting point (P1-5/6) faster than it can be refilled. Growing-season length sets yield potential and dictates variety maturity and sowing date — this is the direct mechanism behind L03's sowing-window and plant-density content, which assumed this timeline without fully deriving it. A late break doesn't just delay sowing, it compresses the season from both ends at once, since terminal drought's timing doesn't move to compensate.

Frost and Heat at the Margins: Crop Consequences

Frost

  • If a radiation frost (P1-7) coincides with flowering, it sterilises cereal florets and can destroy a season's yield in a single night.
  • Frost severity has not eased with warming — clearer skies under a drying climate promote exactly the radiative cooling P1-7 described.

Heat

  • High temperatures during flowering/grain fill damage pollen viability — wheat pollen is impaired above roughly 35°C.
  • Accelerates development (more GDD accumulated per day) and shortens grain fill, shrivelling grain.
Target: ~4 min. This slide is the direct payoff of Part 1's frost-mechanism slide — say that explicitly: "recall the three ingredients for radiation frost; here's what happens when that night lands on a flowering paddock." Land the squeeze plainly: growers want to flower late enough to escape frost but early enough to avoid heat and terminal drought — the optimal flowering window from L03's sowing-time content, now fully explained end to end by today's mechanics. Both frost and heat are threshold, event-driven risks that seasonal averages conceal — a single frost night or heatwave during flowering (a "critical period" in P1-11's sense) can destroy a season's yield even in an otherwise average year, which is exactly why crop models (L02) need to represent daily extremes, not just monthly means, to be useful.

El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO)

  • El Niño — warming of the central/eastern tropical Pacific shifts convection away from Australia; typically drier and hotter across eastern and, more weakly, southern Australia.
  • La Niña — the opposite phase; typically wetter across eastern and northern Australia.
  • Monitored via the SOI (Tahiti–Darwin pressure difference) and sea-surface indices like NINO3.4.

BOM explainer: Understanding ENSO

Target: ~4 min. Qualify ENSO's WA influence carefully — the single most important nuance here: its effect on the south-west grainbelt is real but weaker and less reliable than on eastern Australia, because WA's winter rainfall is governed more by the Indian and Southern Oceans than by the Pacific. That's why an El Niño announcement, though a serious warning for eastern growers, is a less decisive signal for WA — a distinction students need to correctly interpret national media coverage of seasonal outlooks, usually written from an eastern-Australia perspective. ENSO sets up the next slide directly: the IOD is the Indian Ocean driver that matters more here.

The Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD): WA's Dominant Driver

Sea-surface temperature difference between the western (near Africa) and eastern (near Indonesia) tropical Indian Ocean:

  • Positive IOD — cooler water off Indonesia reduces moisture available to systems reaching Australia; typically suppresses winter–spring rainfall, raising drought and frost risk.
  • Negative IOD — warmer eastern Indian Ocean feeds more moisture into Australian systems; typically wetter winters and springs.
  • ENSO and the IOD are physically linked and often coincide — El Niño with a positive IOD compounds dry conditions.

The IOD peaks in exactly WA's winter-spring growing season — a developing positive IOD is a genuine warning of a poor finish

BOM explainer: Indian Ocean Dipole

Target: ~4 min. This is the driver students should associate most strongly with WA — say that explicitly, contrasting directly with the previous slide's ENSO qualification. It matters so much agronomically because of timing: it peaks in the crucial winter-spring period and directly modulates the moisture supply to the systems that deliver WA's growing-season rain (the cold fronts and cloudbands from Part 1's rain-formation slide, and the ridge/fronts slide next). Worst-case framing worth stating plainly: the worst WA droughts occur when multiple drivers align against rainfall at once — a positive IOD compounding with El Niño and a positive SAM (next slide) is the genuine nightmare combination, not any single driver acting alone.

The Southern Annular Mode (SAM)

  • The north–south movement of the belt of westerly winds and their embedded cold fronts circling the Southern Ocean — the systems that actually deliver most of WA's winter rain.
  • Positive SAM — contracts the westerlies poleward (south); in winter this tends to push rain-bearing fronts south of the coastline, reducing rainfall.
  • Negative SAM — expands the westerlies north, bringing fronts further onto the continent, generally increasing winter rainfall.
  • Operates on a much shorter timescale (weeks, not months) than ENSO or the IOD — harder to forecast far ahead, and drives variability within a season.

BOM explainer: Southern Annular Mode

Target: ~3 min. Use the concrete conflict example: a La Niña year is typically wetter for Australia overall, but a positive SAM phase can still push the westerly fronts south of WA, producing a below-average season here even while the national outlook looks wet — real proof these drivers can cancel as easily as reinforce. SAM's short timescale is the key distinguishing feature from ENSO and the IOD — it's why a seasonal outlook can be right on the big picture (a positive IOD year) but still get a particular fortnight wrong. This directly sets up the next slide: SAM's mechanism is that it moves the latitude of the belt carrying the actual rain-delivery systems from Part 1's rain-formation diagram.

The Subtropical Ridge, Cut-Off Lows and Cold Fronts

What actually delivers the rain

  • Subtropical ridge — the high-pressure belt dominating southern Australia in summer, retreating north in winter; its position/strength controls whether fronts can reach the grainbelt.
  • Cold fronts — the workhorses of WA winter rainfall, delivering frequent, moderate falls (the "lift and cool" step from Part 1).

Secondary systems

  • Cut-off lows — less frequent but can deliver heavy, widespread rain.
  • NW cloudbands — draw tropical Indian Ocean moisture across the continent, sometimes producing season-making rains; their moisture supply is modulated by the IOD.
Target: ~3 min. This slide is where the four abstract drivers become physically real: the IOD and ENSO change how much moisture is available to these systems, and SAM changes their latitude, so the indices translate into rain only through their effect on the frequency, track and moisture content of fronts, cut-off lows and cloudbands — literally Part 1's rain-formation diagram, now with the specific WA systems named. A trend toward a stronger, more southerly subtropical ridge is implicated in the long-term drying trend in Part 4 — flag that connection explicitly. This mechanistic understanding is also exactly what makes the frost-risk mechanism from Part 1 make sense synoptically: a front passing through, followed by this ridge building in behind it, brings the clear, dry, calm conditions radiation frost needs.

Seasonal Forecasting: Using the Drivers to Manage Risk

The Bureau of Meteorology issues probabilistic outlooks — the chance of exceeding median rainfall — from dynamical models simulating these drivers forward in time:

  • A "65% chance of a drier-than-median spring" is a shift in the odds, not a certainty — it weights decisions, it doesn't make them.
  • Growers integrate outlooks into nitrogen investment, crop/variety choice and risk exposure — always alongside what the season has actually delivered so far (stored soil moisture from Part 1's water balance, and the timing of the break).
  • Forecast skill is modest, particularly for the autumn break, and declines with lead time.
Target: ~4 min. This slide is the practical payoff of Part 3: the drivers aren't just academic classification, they're the physical basis for the seasonal outlook a grower actually reads before committing to a nitrogen program. Stress the probabilistic framing hard — a student should leave able to correctly interpret a percentage-chance outlook rather than treating it as a yes/no prediction. Note explicitly that "stored soil moisture" here is literally the tank from Part 1's PAW/SMD slide — a grower reading a forecast is combining a probabilistic future outlook with a known, measured current SMD, not relying on the forecast alone. Because forecast skill is modest and declines with lead time, outlooks supplement rather than replace flexible, in-season, tactical management — a direct link forward to the farm financial and risk-management lecture.

Practical Tool: Rainfall Deciles and the DPIRD Rainfall-to-Date Tool

A decile ranks this season's rainfall-to-date against every recorded season at the same location, up to the same date:

  • Decile 1 — among the driest 10% of years on record; decile 5 — the median year; decile 10 — among the wettest 10%.
  • Unlike a seasonal forecast (previous slide, which is probabilistic and about the future), a decile is a measured fact about the past and present — how unusual this season already is, right now.
  • DPIRD's free Rainfall to Date tool lets growers pick any WA weather station and see the current season's decile, updated daily, against the full historical record.
dpird.wa.gov.au → Online Tools → Rainfall and Irrigation Calculators → Rainfall to Date Tool
Target: ~3 min. Pair this deliberately against the previous slide: a seasonal outlook says "the next three months are more likely to be dry"; a decile says "the season so far, right now, is already the 2nd driest on record at this station" — one is a probabilistic forward look, the other is a measured backward look, and a grower uses both together, alongside their own known SMD (Part 1), to make an in-season call. If time and a live internet connection allow, pull the tool up and show a real WA station — the decile framing (1 = driest, 10 = wettest, 5 = median) is the same language used throughout the seasonal-outlook and drought-declaration literature students will encounter in industry, so it's worth them seeing the real interface at least once. This is also a nice bridge to Part 4: a run of consecutive low-decile seasons at a station is exactly the kind of station-level evidence that adds up to the region-wide drying trend covered next.

The Observed Drying Trend in South-West WA

15–20% decline in May–July rainfall since the mid-20th century, accelerating from the 1970s
↓ fronts fewer rain-bearing fronts reaching the region each winter
margins hit hardest decline strongest in the once-most-reliable high-rainfall western/southern margins
Target: ~4 min. This is one of the clearest regional climate-change signals anywhere in the world, and of existential importance to the industry — say that directly, it is not overstatement. The attributed mechanism ties every earlier Part together: a poleward shift and strengthening of the subtropical ridge and storm tracks (linked to a trend toward more positive SAM, and to broader circulation changes from greenhouse warming and, historically, ozone depletion) pushes the rain-bearing westerlies and their fronts further south, away from the continent — literally the same mechanism as a persistent positive-SAM winter (P3-18), just now a multi-decade trend instead of a single season's anomaly. Emphasise this is a measured, sustained trend already reshaping the industry, not a future forecast.

How Growers Have Adapted to a Drier Climate

Counterintuitively, total grain production and water-use efficiency have both risen despite less growing-season rainfall:

  • Conservation agriculture (no-till, stubble retention) conserving scarce soil moisture — protecting the "tank" from Part 1.
  • Earlier and dry sowing to use every millimetre of a shortened season; summer weed control to bank soil moisture ahead of the break.
  • Improved varieties (higher transpiration efficiency, better disease resistance, matched phenology) and responsive nitrogen management.
  • Controlled traffic reducing compaction; shifts in crop mix and geographic distribution of cropping.
The yield gap has closed faster than the climate has dried — but this adaptation has limits
Target: ~4 min. Frame this as a genuine case study in agronomic and technological resilience — every item here is content already met elsewhere in the unit (no-till and conservation agriculture from L01), and this slide's job is to show those weren't independent good ideas, they were the industry's direct, structural response to the drying trend just described. The "yield gap closed faster than the climate has dried" framing is the single sentence worth writing on the board. But land the caution explicitly too: this adaptation has limits, and continued drying will test them — exactly the question the next slide has to confront.

The Future Climate and Its Implications for WA Grain

Consensus projections

  • Continued decline in cool-season rainfall; higher temperatures; more frequent, intense heat extremes at flowering/grain-fill.
  • A shorter effective growing season, partially offset by rising CO₂ boosting water-use efficiency and yield potential for WA's C3 crops.

The net picture

  • CO₂ fertilisation partially but not fully compensates for warming/drying — the balance varies greatly year to year.
  • The dominant consequence is more variability and risk, not a simple uniform decline.
Target: ~4 min. This connects directly to the climate-change crop-model scenarios from L02 — say that explicitly. The strategic responses the industry is pursuing mirror the adaptation list from the previous slide, just projected forward: continued breeding for heat/drought/frost tolerance and shifted phenology, further WUE gains, better seasonal forecasting, and possible shifts in crop mix and the spatial extent of viable cropping. Close Part 4 on the unit's throughline stated plainly: a drying, more variable, more extreme climate makes the disciplined, systems-based, risk-aware agronomy taught in this unit more essential, not less, to the future of WA grain production.
