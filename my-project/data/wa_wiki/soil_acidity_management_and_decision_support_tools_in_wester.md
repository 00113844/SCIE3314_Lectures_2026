# Soil Acidity Management and Decision Support Tools in Western Australia

## Summary
Soil acidity is a primary environmental constraint in Western Australian cropping systems, leading to significant yield penalties through nutrient deficiency and aluminum toxicity. The implementation of precision decision-support tools, specifically the iLime app, allows growers to model long-term pH trajectories and economic returns to optimize lime application and incorporation strategies.

## Key Facts
- **Economic Impact:** Soil acidification is estimated to cost Western Australian agriculture between $500 million and $1,500 million annually in lost income.
- **Critical pH Thresholds:** Topsoil (0–10 cm) pH below 5.5 limits nutrient availability, while subsoil pH below 4.8 triggers the release of toxic aluminum, which severely inhibits root growth.
- **The iLime Tool:** Developed through a collaboration between the Department of Primary Industries and Regional Development (DPIRD) and the Department of Water and Environment Regulation (DWER), iLime uses the Oplime engine to predict pH changes, yield responses, and profitability over a 20-year horizon.
- **Predictive Accuracy:** Validation against long-term field trials demonstrates a high correlation between predicted and observed pH changes, with an R-squared value of 0.8.
- **Agronomic Trade-offs:** While liming neutralizes acidity, it can induce manganese deficiency (requiring foliar mitigation) and increase the prevalence of snails and take-all disease.
- **Management Trends:** Recent data from the Avon River Basin indicates a positive trend in soil health, with the proportion of topsoil below pH 5.5 decreasing from 72% to 46%.

## Local Examples
- **Avon River Basin:** A key focus area for nutrient management and environmental impact studies, encompassing approximately 60% of the WA wheat belt.
- **Regional Testing:** Sensibility testing of iLime outputs was conducted with farmers in the Korda and south coast regions to ensure alignment with on-farm experience.
- **Deep Soil Re-engineering:** Experimental trials involving the mixing of lime to depths of 70–90 cm have shown potential to double yields, though high costs currently limit widespread adoption.
