# Cereal Crop Development and Yield Potential in Western Australia

## Summary
This topic examines the physiological development of cereal crops and the mathematical determination of yield potential within Western Australian dryland systems. It emphasizes the GxExM (Genotype $\times$ Environment $\times$ Management) interaction, focusing on how precision management can close the "yield gap" between actual farm production and theoretical potential.

## Key Facts
- **Crop Development Phases**: Cereal growth progresses through five distinct stages: germination (0–20 days after sowing), establishment (20–30 days), early growth (30–40 days), potential development (40–60 days), and realization (after 90 days).
- **Yield Potential Calculation**: Potential yield is estimated using the formula: $\text{Rainfall} - \text{Losses (evaporation, runoff, drainage)} \times \text{Water Use Efficiency (WUE)}$.
- **Water Use Efficiency (WUE)**: Transpiration efficiency varies by species:
    - Wheat: 20 kg/ha/mm
    - Barley/Oats: 25 kg/ha/mm
    - Pulses: 15 kg/ha/mm
    - Canola: 12 kg/ha/mm (lower due to energy diversion into oil production).
- **The Yield Gap**: While WA wheat crops currently achieve 50–60% of their potential yield (up from 30% in the 1980s), a gap remains that can be addressed through tactical management (seeding timing, N application) and strategic management (addressing subsoil acidity and compaction).
- **Nitrogen Immobilization**: Residues with a high Carbon-to-Nitrogen (C:N) ratio (e.g., cereals at 30–100) cause microbes to scavenge soil nitrogen, leading to "nitrogen lockup." Legume residues (C:N 20–25) mitigate this effect.
- **Yield Estimation**: Field yields are estimated by calculating: $\text{Heads/m}^2 \times \text{Spikelets/head} \times \text{Kernels/spikelet} \times \text{Average grain weight (approx. 35 mg)}$.

## Local Examples
- **Farmanko**: Utilized as a data source to analyze variability in how different WA farmers achieve potential yield.
- **Historical Yield Trends**: WA wheat yields have increased since the mid-1970s due to the integration of phosphorus fertilization, no-till technology, and improved legume pastures.
