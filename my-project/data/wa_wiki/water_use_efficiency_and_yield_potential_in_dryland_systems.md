# Water Use Efficiency and Yield Potential in Dryland Systems

## Summary
Water Use Efficiency (WUE) serves as the primary benchmark for crop productivity in the southern Australian grain belt, where water is the limiting factor for yield. In precision agriculture, understanding the gap between actual and potential WUE allows growers to implement targeted agronomic interventions to minimize soil evaporation and maximize "crop per drop."

## Key Facts
- **WUE Benchmark:** The theoretical potential for wheat in dryland environments is established at 20 kg/ha/mm of water used.
- **Water Use Calculation:** Total water use is determined by the formula: (Soil water at sowing - Soil water at harvest) + Rainfall - (Drainage + Runoff).
- **The Yield Gap:** Actual WUE in Western Australia often falls significantly below the 20 kg/ha/mm benchmark due to constraints such as nutrient deficiencies (N, P, Cu), pests (cereal cyst nematodes), and suboptimal sowing dates.
- **Evaporation Management:** Soil evaporation is a major loss component; agronomic practices such as stubble retention and the use of nitrogen to promote rapid canopy closure can significantly reduce these losses.
- **Nutrient Trade-offs:** While increased nitrogen application generally improves WUE by enhancing root systems and biomass, it often results in a decrease in Nitrogen Use Efficiency (NUE).
- **Genetic Gains:** Plant breeding has historically increased wheat WUE through the development of semi-dwarf varieties and improvements in the harvest index.

## Local Examples
- **Regional Performance:** Data from Meriden and Morava indicate actual WUE slopes of 7.72 and 4.93 kg/ha/mm, respectively, highlighting the significant gap compared to the 20 kg/ha/mm potential.
- **Nautilus System:** A machinery application used to enhance WUE by reducing soil disturbance and enabling precise, deep fertilizer placement.
- **Crop Sequences:** Lupin-wheat rotations in WA demonstrate superior WUE compared to continuous wheat cropping systems.
