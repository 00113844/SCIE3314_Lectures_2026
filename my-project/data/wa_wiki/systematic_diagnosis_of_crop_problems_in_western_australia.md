# Systematic Diagnosis of Crop Problems in Western Australia

## Summary
This guide outlines a systematic methodology for diagnosing agronomic stressors in Western Australian cropping systems, emphasizing the integration of plant symptomatology, root health analysis, and field-scale spatial clues. In precision agriculture, accurate diagnosis is essential to differentiate between nutrient deficiencies, biotic pathogens, and chemical toxicity to ensure targeted and cost-effective interventions.

## Key Facts
- **Symptom Categorization**: Plant issues are primarily classified as necrosis (actual tissue death/lesions) or chlorosis (loss of chlorophyll/color change).
- **Nutrient Mobility**: The location of symptoms indicates nutrient mobility; mobile nutrients (e.g., Nitrogen) manifest deficiencies in older leaves, while immobile nutrients affect younger growth.
- **Herbicide Impact**: Canola is highly sensitive to Group B sulfonylureas (e.g., metsulfuron), which compress growing points leading to "strappy" leaves and compressed flowering heads.
- **Root Diagnostics**: Root morphology is a critical diagnostic tool:
    - **Rhizoctonia root rot**: Characterized by spear-tipped roots.
    - **Cereal cyst nematodes**: Identified by knotted roots and white egg masses.
    - **Trifluralin (Group D) damage**: Results in stubby, spade-tipped roots and thickened coleoptiles.
- **No-Till Interaction**: No-till farming practices can exacerbate Trifluralin damage due to reduced soil mixing and increased seed contact with herbicide residues.
- **Spatial Paddock Clues**: Diagnosis must include "paddock clues" such as boom spray patterns (herbicide drift), waterlogging zones, and edge-of-paddock effects (aphid-borne viruses).

## Local Examples
- **Aphid Vectors**: In Western Australia, aphid invasions carrying viruses (e.g., Bean Yellow Mosaic Virus) typically enter paddocks from the west or north.
- **Disease Shifts**: Rhizoctonia root rot has become a more dominant issue in WA wheat as the prevalence of Take-all has declined due to improved grass control.
- **Lupin Pathology**: Brown leaf spot in narrow-leaf lupins is commonly spread via rain splash of soil-borne spores affecting older leaves.
