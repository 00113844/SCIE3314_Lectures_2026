# Yield Gap and Water Use Efficiency in WA

## Overview
The yield gap represents the disparity between the theoretical potential yield—achievable under ideal conditions with water as the only limiting factor—and the actual farm-gate yield. In the water-limited environments of Western Australia, this gap is primarily quantified through Water Use Efficiency (WUE), making it the central benchmark for precision agronomic interventions.

## Key Themes
- **The WUE Benchmark:** The theoretical potential for wheat in WA is established at 20 kg of grain per hectare per millimeter (mm) of water used, with genetic breeding targets aiming to push this toward 25 kg/ha/mm.
- **Water Loss Dynamics:** Approximately one-third (100–110 mm) of growing season rainfall is typically lost to non-productive sources, primarily soil evaporation. This loss is a critical driver of the yield gap.
- **Drivers of Yield Variance:** While environment (rainfall and temperature) is the primary driver of variance, management accounts for up to 36% of the gap. Notably, 70% of historical yield gains in WA are attributed to management improvements rather than genetic advances.
- **Tactical vs. Strategic Management:** Yield gap closure is pursued through tactical interventions (seasonal adjustments to seed rate and nitrogen application) and strategic interventions (long-term improvements in soil fertility and compaction management).
- **Nitrogen Trade-offs:** Nitrogen application is used to reduce the yield gap by promoting rapid canopy closure (which minimizes soil evaporation) and enhancing root systems; however, this often creates an inverse relationship where increased WUE leads to decreased Nitrogen Use Efficiency (NUE).
- **Rainfall-Dependent Gap Variance:** The yield gap is typically narrower in low-rainfall areas (<250 mm) where crops utilize a higher proportion of available water, and wider in high-rainfall areas where water losses are more pronounced.

## Regional Patterns
Actual farm efficiency varies significantly across the WA wheatbelt, with some operations achieving 60–80% of their potential yield while others reach only 30%. Regional data from sites like Meriden and Morava demonstrate actual WUE slopes (7.72 and 4.93 kg/ha/mm, respectively) that fall far below the 20 kg/ha/mm theoretical potential.
