# Crop Rotation and Sequence Management in Western Australia

## Summary
Crop rotation in Western Australia involves the strategic cycling of cereals, legumes, and oilseeds to manage soil-borne diseases, optimize nutrient availability, and control weed populations. In semi-arid WA environments, the selection of rotation sequences is a critical precision management decision that balances agronomic benefits—such as nitrogen fixation and moisture conservation—against economic risks and drought tolerance.

## Key Facts
- **Nutrient Dynamics:** Legume integration provides a significant yield boost to subsequent wheat (average 49%) by fixing approximately 37 kg N/ha and providing residues with low carbon-to-nitrogen (C:N) ratios, which prevents nitrogen immobilization.
- **Disease Suppression:** Rotations are used to break the cycles of cereal-specific pathogens like take-all and crown rot; however, some pathogens like Rhizoctonia have wide host ranges that make rotation-based control challenging.
- **Water Management:** Fallow efficiency in WA is relatively low (20–30%) due to high evaporation; however, managed fallows can increase soil nitrogen through mineralization (averaging 71 kg N/ha).
- **No-Till Synergy:** Conservation agriculture (no-till) supports rotation diversity by conserving soil moisture and reducing the incidence of cereal cyst nematodes.
- **Phosphorus Mobilization:** Specific break crops, namely chickpeas and lupins, enhance phosphorus availability by secreting organic acids (citrate and malate) into the rhizosphere.
- **Weed Control:** Diverse rotations allow for the use of varied herbicide modes of action, though certain crops like lupins offer fewer chemical control options, increasing the risk of weed buildup.

## Local Examples
- **Yield Data:** Research by Kierkegaard and Angus demonstrates that wheat yields are generally higher following break crops than in continuous wheat systems.
- **Soil Texture Variance:** Fallow water storage in WA varies significantly by soil type, with clay soils storing an average of 38 mm (max 125 mm) compared to sandy soils which average 9 mm (max 38 mm).
- **Common WA Sequences:** Typical rotations include cycles of wheat, lupin, and canola.
