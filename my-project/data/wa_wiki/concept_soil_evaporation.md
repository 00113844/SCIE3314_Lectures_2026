# Soil Evaporation and Water Use Efficiency in WA

## Overview
Water Use Efficiency (WUE) is the primary benchmark for crop productivity in the Mediterranean-type environment of the Western Australian grain belt, where rainfall is the fundamental limiting factor for yield. In this context, soil evaporation represents a critical non-productive water loss that must be minimized to maximize the "crop per drop" and bridge the gap between actual and theoretical yield potential.

## Key Themes
- **The WUE Benchmark and Yield Gap:** The theoretical upper limit for wheat WUE is established at 20–25 kg of grain per hectare per millimeter (mm) of water used. However, actual field performance in WA often falls significantly below this (e.g., 4.93 to 7.72 kg/ha/mm), creating a "yield gap" driven by nutrient deficiencies, pests, and suboptimal management.
- **Critical Evaporative Thresholds:** Soil evaporation is a major component of water loss, with some estimates placing this loss at approximately 110 mm. When available soil water falls below this evaporative threshold, crop yields typically become negligible.
- **Canopy Closure Strategies:** To mitigate evaporative loss, precision agronomy focuses on rapid canopy closure. This is achieved through early sowing dates (e.g., April in WA) and strategic nitrogen application, which promotes robust root systems and faster biomass accumulation to shade the soil surface.
- **Conservation Tillage and Soil Management:** Stubble retention and no-till practices are essential for reducing soil evaporation. Advanced systems, such as the Nautilus system, further enhance WUE by reducing soil disturbance and enabling precise, deep fertilizer placement.
- **Rotational Synergy:** The integration of pulses into cropping sequences improves the WUE of subsequent cereal crops. For instance, wheat grown following lupins demonstrates superior WUE and lower incidences of disease (e.g., Take-all) compared to continuous wheat systems.
- **Genetic and Physiological Drivers:** WUE has been historically improved through the breeding of semi-dwarf wheat varieties and improvements in the harvest index. Physiologically, C3 plants like wheat are less transpiration-efficient than C4 plants, while oilseeds and pulses generally exhibit lower efficiency due to higher protein and oil concentrations.

## Regional Patterns
Data across the WA wheatbelt, specifically from regions such as Merredin, Wongan Hills, and Morava, consistently demonstrate a significant disparity between theoretical WUE potential and actual regional performance, highlighting the pervasive impact of environmental constraints and management inefficiencies.
