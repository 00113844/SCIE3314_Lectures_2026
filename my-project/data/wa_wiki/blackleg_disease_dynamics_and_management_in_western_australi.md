# Blackleg Disease Dynamics and Management in Western Australian Canola

## Summary
Blackleg is a devastating fungal disease affecting canola in Western Australia, characterized by a polycyclic infection cycle driven by the synergistic effect of windborne ascospores and rain-splashed conidia. Effective management in WA's Mediterranean climate requires an integrated approach combining resistant cultivars, strategic fungicide application, and the use of precision forecasting tools to mitigate significant yield losses.

## Key Facts
- **Polycyclic Nature:** Unlike many global regions where blackleg is monocyclic, WA epidemics are polycyclic, involving both primary ascospore infections from stubbles and secondary conidial infections via pycnidia.
- **Infection Mechanism:** Ascospores land on cotyledons and leaves to initiate infection, while rain-splashed conidia spread the disease further, significantly increasing severity when both spore types affect a single plant.
- **Stubble Persistence:** Due to minimum tillage practices, over 1.5 million hectares of canola stubbles remain infective for multiple years; one-year-old stubbles produce the highest volume of ascospores.
- **Environmental Susceptibility:** Plant vulnerability is increased by low soil nutrient levels, wind-induced sandblasting (physical damage), and late-season drought stress.
- **Precision Forecasting:** The Blackleg CM decision support tool utilizes temperature and rainfall data to predict pseudothecial maturity, allowing growers to optimize sowing windows.
- **Fungicide Interaction:** The economic return on fungicides is highest for varieties with intermediate resistance (ranking 4–5); highly resistant varieties (ranking 7) show negligible yield benefit from treatment.

## Local Examples
- **Regional Variation:** In the Mount Barker area, ascospore release typically occurs over 2–4 months starting in mid-March, whereas in East Chapman, warmer temperatures delay release until July.
- **Cultivar Performance:** Hyola 42 is noted as highly susceptible, often suffering near-total loss near one-year-old stubbles, while the Dunkeld variety demonstrates superior resistance.
- **Economic Impact:** Blackleg causes estimated annual losses of up to $40 million within Western Australia.
