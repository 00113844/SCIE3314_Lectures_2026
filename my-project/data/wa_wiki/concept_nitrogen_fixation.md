# Biological Nitrogen Fixation in WA Cropping Systems

## Overview
Biological Nitrogen Fixation (BNF) is the symbiotic process by which specific legume crops, in association with *Rhizobium* bacteria, convert atmospheric nitrogen ($\text{N}_2$) into plant-available ammonia. In Western Australia's Mediterranean dryland systems, BNF is a critical tool for enhancing soil fertility, reducing reliance on synthetic nitrogen fertilizers, and improving the overall water use efficiency (WUE) of the cropping sequence.

## Key Themes
- **Symbiotic Inoculation and Persistence**: Effective BNF requires the presence of compatible rhizobia (e.g., Group G for lupins). While these bacteria persist in the soil, re-inoculation is recommended every 5–7 years to prevent "genetic drift" or mixing with less efficient indigenous strains that can reduce fixation rates.
- **Nutritional Synergies and Inhibitors**: BNF is highly dependent on specific micronutrients; molybdenum is essential for the nitrogenase enzyme, while phosphorus is critical for nodule development. Conversely, the application of synthetic nitrogen fertilizer can inhibit rhizobia effectiveness, rendering it unnecessary and counterproductive for established legumes.
- **The "Legume Effect" on Cereal Yields**: Integrating BNF-capable pulses into rotations provides a significant nitrogen credit for subsequent crops. This "legume effect" can increase following wheat yields by 86–203% and drastically reduce the prevalence of root diseases, such as reducing Take-all infection from 54% to 15%.
- **Species-Environment Matching**: The efficiency of BNF is mediated by soil chemistry and climate. Narrowleaf lupins are optimized for the acidic sandy soils prevalent in the WA Wheatbelt, whereas Albus lupins are utilized in alkaline soils, and faba beans are reserved for high-pH, heavy clay soils in high-rainfall zones.
- **Systemic Sustainability and WUE**: Pulse integration reduces the overall carbon footprint of the cropping system by 20–35%. Furthermore, legumes improve system-wide Water Use Efficiency, providing a high-protein yield with significantly lower water requirements compared to animal-based protein sources.
- **Drought Adaptation Mechanisms**: While BNF is energy-intensive, certain pulses (field peas, lentils, and chickpeas) employ osmotic adjustment to maintain productivity and nitrogen fixation under severe water stress, outperforming faba beans and lupins in low-rainfall environments.

## Regional Patterns
BNF strategies in WA are strictly partitioned by soil type, with a heavy reliance on Narrowleaf lupins in the acidic sandplains and a strategic shift toward chickpeas and field peas in the northern, lower-rainfall districts.
