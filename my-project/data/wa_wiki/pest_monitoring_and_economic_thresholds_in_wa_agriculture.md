# Pest Monitoring and Economic Thresholds in WA Agriculture

## Summary
Effective pest management in Western Australian cropping systems relies on the transition from qualitative observation to quantitative decision-making using Economic Injury Levels (EIL) and Economic Thresholds (ET). In the variable climate of WA, these thresholds are dynamic, requiring precise monitoring to balance the cost of chemical intervention against potential yield losses.

## Key Facts
- **Injury vs. Damage**: A critical distinction is made between *injury* (the physical effect of a pest on a plant or animal) and *damage* (the measurable loss in production, such as kg/ha).
- **Economic Injury Level (EIL)**: The lowest pest population density that will cause economic damage, defined as the point where the cost of control equals the value of the yield saved. It is calculated as: $EIL = C / (V \times I \times D)$, where $C$ is control cost, $V$ is market value, $I$ is injury units per insect, and $D$ is damage per injury unit.
- **Economic Threshold (ET)**: The pest density at which control measures should be implemented to prevent the population from reaching the EIL.
- **Environmental Influence**: EIL and ET are not static; for example, in wet years, crops may tolerate higher pest densities with less yield loss compared to dry years.
- **Monitoring Methodologies**: 
    - *Qualitative*: Presence/absence monitoring, primarily used for biosecurity.
    - *Quantitative*: Population density tracking used to predict trends and trigger ET.
- **Resistance Management**: To prevent the rapid evolution of insecticide resistance (as seen historically with DDT and Warfarin), modern Integrated Pest Management (IPM) requires the rotation of different modes of action.
- **Biocontrol Dynamics**: Parasitoids are most effective at low pest densities for specific targets, whereas generalist predators are more suited for high-density scenarios but often struggle in WA dryland systems due to lack of food during non-crop periods.

## Local Examples
- **Biosecurity Monitoring**: Australia maintains over 100 sentinel chicken sites, with more than 30 located in Western Australia, to monitor for avian diseases.
- **Microbial Control**: The CSIRO developed *Metarhizium*, a fungal agent that has become a leading global tool for locust control.
- **Vertebrate Management**: The use of myxomatosis and rabbit haemorrhagic disease (calicivirus) to drastically reduce rabbit populations across southern Australia.
- **Livestock Pests**: Face flies on cattle, which act as vectors for the bacterial disease pinkeye.
