# Climate Change Impacts on Crop Pathogen Epidemiology

## Overview
In Western Australian (WA) cropping systems, climate change is characterized by a significant drying trend—with growing season rainfall declining to 85–95% of historical levels—and rising mean temperatures. These shifts alter the epidemiology of crop pathogens by modifying spore release timing, pathogen viability on stubble, and the physiological resistance of the host plants.

## Key Themes
- **Pathogen "Winners" and "Losers":** Rising temperatures are shifting the prevalence of specific diseases. "Loser" pathogens include clover scorch and stripe rust, which favor cooler, wetter conditions. Conversely, "winner" scenarios include an increased risk of catastrophic stem rust epidemics as temperatures rise.
- **Strategic Sowing Windows:** Climate shifts can create agronomic opportunities. For field peas, predictive modeling suggests that pea black spot ascospore release is occurring earlier, potentially expanding the viable sowing window by up to five weeks and reducing overall yield loss.
- **Rhizoctonia Vulnerability:** Rhizoctonia bare patch is exacerbated by the WA drying trend. Low rainfall and cold soil temperatures impede rapid root growth, increasing plant vulnerability, while increased summer rainfall can support the weed hosts that maintain the pathogen.
- **Stubble Persistence and Decomposition:** The dry summers of southwestern WA facilitate the long-term carryover of pathogens; for example, blackleg spores can remain viable on stubble for up to four years. However, an increase in summer rainfall may accelerate residue breakdown, potentially allowing for tighter crop rotations.
- **Temperature-Sensitive Resistance:** Host resistance is not static; in canola, both major gene and quantitative resistance to blackleg are temperature-sensitive and may fail when temperatures exceed 13–15°C.
- **Precision Predictive Modeling:** To mitigate climate-driven risks, WA agriculture relies on weather-based simulation tools (e.g., the Blackleg Manager and sporacle models). These tools provide the temporal accuracy required to optimize sowing dates and variety selection.

## Regional Patterns
Pathogen dynamics vary significantly across the WA wheatbelt; northern regions like East Chapman typically experience longer pre-hazard periods, whereas southern regions like Mount Barker face earlier spore maturation and more prolonged hazard periods.
