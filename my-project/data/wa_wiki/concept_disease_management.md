# Integrated Pathogen Management in WA Cropping Systems

## Overview
Integrated pathogen management in Western Australia (WA) is the strategic coordination of genetic resistance, crop rotation, and precision forecasting to mitigate yield losses in a Mediterranean climate. This approach is specifically necessitated by the prevalence of no-till farming, which increases the persistence of stubble-borne inoculum, and a drying climate trend that alters traditional disease epidemiology.

## Key Themes
- **Stubble-Borne Inoculum Persistence:** The adoption of minimum tillage has created vast reservoirs of infective crop residues. Pathogens such as Blackleg and Sclerotinia exhibit extreme longevity in WA's sandy soils—persisting for several years to a decade—rendering traditional short-term rotations insufficient for complete eradication.
- **Climate-Driven Epidemiological Shifts:** Rising temperatures and declining growing-season rainfall are redefining "winner" and "loser" diseases. While warmer, drier conditions have reduced the prevalence of Clover Scorch, they may increase the risk of catastrophic stem rust and alter the spore release windows for Pea Black Spot, potentially expanding viable sowing windows.
- **Polycyclic Infection Dynamics:** Unlike many global contexts, certain WA diseases exhibit polycyclic behavior. Blackleg, for example, utilizes a synergistic combination of windborne ascospores (primary infection) and rain-splashed conidia (secondary infection), which significantly amplifies disease severity.
- **Precision Forecasting and Decision Support:** To optimize sowing dates and fungicide applications, WA agriculture relies on weather-based simulation tools (e.g., Blackleg CM and sporacle models). These tools translate temperature and rainfall data into actionable windows to avoid peak ascospore release.
- **Limitations of Chemical and Cultural Control:** High application costs and the difficulty of timing sprays make fungicides economically volatile, particularly for Sclerotinia. Furthermore, broad-host pathogens like Rhizoctonia resist rotation-based control, shifting the management priority toward the breeding of genetically resistant cultivars.
- **Temperature-Sensitivity of Resistance:** Genetic resistance is not absolute; both quantitative and major gene resistance in canola can fail when environmental thresholds (e.g., 13–15°C) are exceeded, highlighting the interaction between genotype and climate.

## Regional Patterns
Pathogen dynamics vary significantly across the wheatbelt; for instance, southern regions like Mount Barker experience early spore maturation and prolonged hazard periods, whereas northern regions like East Chapman face delayed release due to temperature differentials.
