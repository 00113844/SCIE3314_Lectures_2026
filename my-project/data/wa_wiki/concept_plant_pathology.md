# Integrated Crop Pathology and Disease Management in WA

## Overview
Crop pathology in Western Australia involves the systematic study and mitigation of biotic stressors—fungi, viruses, and nematodes—within a Mediterranean climate characterized by a significant drying trend. In the context of precision agriculture, this requires an integrated approach that combines multi-scale diagnostics, predictive epidemiological modeling, and adaptive management to maintain yield stability across the wheatbelt.

## Key Themes
- **Climate-Driven Epidemiological Shifts**: Rising temperatures and declining growing-season rainfall (now 85–95% of historical levels) are altering pathogen prevalence. This is evidenced by the increased incidence of Fusarium crown rot in drier eastern belts and a corresponding decrease in Septoria nodorum blotch.
- **Multi-Scale Diagnostic Framework**: Effective pathology management relies on a hierarchical diagnostic process: identifying spatial "paddock clues" (e.g., boom spray patterns or west-to-east aphid incursions), analyzing foliar symptoms (necrosis vs. chlorosis), and performing root morphology assessments (e.g., spear-tipped roots for Rhizoctonia or knotted roots for cereal cyst nematodes).
- **Predictive Precision Management**: To mitigate "loser" diseases and optimize sowing windows, WA producers utilize predictive tools such as the blackleg sporacle model. These tools allow for high temporal accuracy in timing fungicide applications and sowing dates to avoid peak spore release.
- **Pathogen Resistance and Chemical Efficacy**: The emergence of resistance to key fungicide groups (strobilurins, DMI, and SDHI) in pathogens like powdery mildew and barley net blotch necessitates a shift toward Integrated Pest Management (IPM), emphasizing resistant cultivars and strategic crop rotations.
- **Soil-Borne Pathogen and Residue Dynamics**: The interaction between no-till farming and soil-borne pathogens is critical; while no-till preserves soil structure, it can exacerbate herbicide residues (e.g., Trifluralin) and maintain inoculum for pathogens like Rhizoctonia and Take-all.

## Regional Patterns
Pathogen movement in WA exhibits strong directional patterns, with cereal rusts and aphid-borne viruses typically migrating from west to north/east. Additionally, there is significant latitudinal variation in disease hazards, such as the prolonged blackleg hazard period in Southern WA (Mount Barker) compared to the longer pre-hazard window in Northern WA (East Chapman).
