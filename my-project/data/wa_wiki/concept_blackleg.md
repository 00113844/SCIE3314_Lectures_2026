# Integrated Management of Blackleg in WA Canola

## Overview
Blackleg, caused by the fungus *Leptosphaeria maculans*, is a critical polycyclic disease of canola in Western Australia. Its management is uniquely challenging due to the synergy between the region's Mediterranean climate, the prevalence of minimum-tillage systems that preserve inoculum, and the high evolutionary rate of the pathogen within the WA wheatbelt.

## Key Themes
- **Polycyclic Infection Cycle:** Unlike monocyclic models seen in other global regions, WA blackleg epidemics are driven by both primary infections (windborne ascospores from stubble) and secondary infections (rain-splashed conidia from pycnidia), which significantly amplify disease severity.
- **Stubble Inoculum Dynamics:** Minimum tillage practices preserve infested canola stubbles, which remain infective for several years. One-year-old stubbles are identified as the most prolific source of ascospores.
- **Enhanced Spore Mobility:** Pathogen dispersal in Western Australia is significantly more extensive than in European systems, with windborne ascospores capable of traveling up to 15 km, thereby expanding the risk zone for new crops.
- **Environmental and Nutritional Susceptibility:** The biological buffering capacity of canola is reduced by the nutrient-deficient, weathered soils typical of the region. Additionally, physical damage from wind-induced sandblasting and late-season drought stress increase plant vulnerability.
- **Precision Sowing and Forecasting:** Management has shifted toward precision timing using decision support tools (e.g., Blackleg CM). By forecasting pseudothecial maturity based on temperature and rainfall, growers can adjust sowing windows to avoid peak ascospore release.
- **Genetic Resistance and Fungicide Interaction:** There is a non-linear relationship between cultivar resistance and chemical efficacy; fungicides provide the highest economic return for varieties with intermediate resistance, while highly resistant varieties derive negligible benefit.
- **Evolutionary Hotspot:** Western Australia is a global center for *L. maculans* evolution, originating 13 of the 26 known global races, which necessitates continuous breeding for new resistant germplasm.

## Regional Patterns
Spore release timing varies significantly across the state, with Mount Barker experiencing release over a 2–4 month window starting in mid-March, whereas warmer conditions in East Chapman delay release until July.
