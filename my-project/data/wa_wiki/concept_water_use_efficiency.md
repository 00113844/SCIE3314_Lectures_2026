# Water Use Efficiency in WA Dryland Systems

## Overview
Water Use Efficiency (WUE) is the primary diagnostic metric for crop productivity in Western Australia's Mediterranean dryland systems, defined as the mass of grain produced per unit of water transpired and evaporated (kg/ha/mm). In a region where rainfall is the fundamental limiting factor, maximizing WUE is the central objective of precision agronomy to bridge the "yield gap" between actual farm production and theoretical biological potential.

## Key Themes
- **The WUE Benchmark and the Yield Gap**: The theoretical upper limit for wheat WUE in Australia is established at 20–25 kg/ha/mm. However, actual regional performance often falls significantly short; for example, data from Morawa and Merredin show actual WUE slopes as low as 4.93 to 7.72 kg/ha/mm, indicating a substantial yield gap driven by nutrient deficiencies, pests, and suboptimal management.
- **Evaporation Mitigation**: Soil evaporation is a critical loss component, estimated at approximately 110 mm in some systems. WUE is optimized by reducing these losses through stubble retention, no-till practices, and early dry seeding (April–June), which facilitate rapid canopy closure to shade the soil surface.
- **Rotational Synergy and Pulse Integration**: Integrating pulses (particularly lupins) into cereal rotations enhances system-wide WUE. This is achieved by improving soil nitrogen levels and suppressing soil-borne pathogens; for instance, wheat following lupins shows a reduction in Take-all disease incidence from 54% to approximately 15–19%.
- **Physiological and Genetic Drivers**: WUE varies by species and morphology. While barley/oats exhibit higher efficiency (25 kg/ha/mm) than wheat (20 kg/ha/mm) or canola (12 kg/ha/mm), genetic gains in wheat have been realized through the development of semi-dwarf varieties and an improved harvest index.
- **Nutrient-Water Interactions**: There is a complex trade-off between Nitrogen Use Efficiency (NUE) and WUE. While optimal nitrogen application promotes more robust root systems and faster canopy development (increasing WUE), excessive application can lead to a decrease in NUE.
- **Precision Soil Management**: The integration of Controlled Traffic Farming (CTF) and no-till systems protects the crop zone from compaction, improving water infiltration and reducing the oxidation of organic matter, which further stabilizes the water-holding capacity of the soil.

## Regional Patterns
Across the WA wheatbelt, WUE is heavily influenced by a north-south gradient; northern regions (e.g., Geraldton) experience faster crop development due to higher temperatures, while southern regions (e.g., Borden) benefit from cooler conditions and longer growing seasons. A consistent pattern across all regions is the reliance on no-till and dry seeding to capture early-season moisture.
