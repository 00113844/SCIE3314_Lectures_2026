# Integrated Weed Management in Western Australian Cropping

## Overview
Integrated Weed Management (IWM) in Western Australia is a holistic strategic framework designed to maintain weed seed banks at sustainable levels while mitigating the escalation of herbicide resistance. In the WA context, IWM is distinguished by its heavy reliance on the biological decay of seeds in no-till systems and the integration of high-tech mechanical interventions during harvest.

## Key Themes
- **Seed Bank Dynamics and Depletion**: Management strategies are tailored to species-specific biology; for example, annual ryegrass (*Lolium rigidum*) seeds decay rapidly on the soil surface in no-till systems, meaning three consecutive years of effective control can nearly eliminate the seed bank. In contrast, wild radish exhibits higher dormancy and staggered germination, requiring more persistent intervention.
- **Diversification of Chemical Control**: To combat target-site and metabolic resistance, IWM emphasizes the rotation of Herbicide Modes of Action (MOA)—such as Group 1 (ACCase), Group 2 (ALS), and Group 15 (pyroxasulfone). This includes the "double knock" approach, utilizing a systemic herbicide followed by a non-systemic one to eliminate survivors.
- **Precision Application Technology**: The transition from pressure-based spraying to Pulse Width Modulation (PWM) allows for constant nozzle pressure and uniform droplet size regardless of vehicle speed, reducing drift. Additionally, sensor-based systems like WeedSeeker utilize chlorophyll detection to enable targeted spot-spraying, significantly reducing total chemical volume.
- **Harvest Weed Seed Control (HWSC)**: To break the cycle of seed return to the soil, WA systems employ mechanical and thermal interventions, including narrow windrow burning, chaff carts, and seed destructors (e.g., Harrington Seed Destructor), which can physically destroy up to 95% of weed seeds.
- **Cultural and Biological Competition**: Crop selection is used as a weed suppression tool, with barley identified as the most competitive cereal due to its superior tillering and early canopy closure, followed by wheat, while grain legumes provide the least competition.
- **Predictive Modelling and Data Integration**: Decision-making is increasingly driven by simulation tools such as APSIM and the Ryegrass Integrated Management (RIM) tool, which model long-term resistance trends and optimize the timing of pre- and post-emergent applications.

## Regional Patterns
WA cropping is characterized by exceptionally high herbicide intensity (averaging 5.4 sprays per year), which has accelerated the development of cross-resistance in outcrossing species, necessitating a shift from purely chemical reliance to integrated mechanical and biological strategies.
