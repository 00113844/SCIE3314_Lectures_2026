# Water Use Efficiency and Pulse Integration in Western Australian Dryland Systems

## Summary
In the Mediterranean-type environment of southern Australia, rainfall is the primary limiting factor for crop productivity. Water Use Efficiency (WUE) is a critical metric for evaluating yield per unit of water used, and maximizing this efficiency through precision agronomy and strategic crop rotations is essential for bridging the yield gap in dryland systems.

## Key Facts
- **WUE Benchmarks:** The theoretical upper limit for WUE in wheat is approximately 20–25 kg of grain per hectare per millimeter (mm) of water used.
- **Evaporative Loss:** Soil evaporation represents a significant water loss (estimated at ~110 mm in some studies); yields typically become negligible when available water falls below this threshold.
- **Management Strategies:** WUE is improved by reducing soil evaporation through stubble retention, no-till practices, and early sowing (e.g., April in WA) to achieve rapid canopy closure.
- **Nutrient Interaction:** Optimal nitrogen application enhances WUE by promoting more robust root systems and faster canopy development, though excessive nitrogen can lead to inefficiencies.
- **Rotational Benefits:** Integrating pulses into rotations improves the WUE of subsequent cereal crops; for example, wheat grown after lupins exhibits higher WUE than continuous wheat systems.
- **Physiological Efficiency:** C4 plants (e.g., maize) generally possess higher transpiration efficiency than C3 plants (e.g., wheat), while oilseeds and pulses typically have lower efficiency due to higher protein and oil concentrations.

## Local Examples
- **Regional Performance:** Historical data from Merredin and Wongan Hills indicated actual WUE slopes of 4.93 to 7.72 kg/ha/mm, significantly lower than the theoretical potential of 20 kg/ha/mm.
- **Disease Suppression:** In WA rotations, wheat following lupins showed a Take-all incidence of 19%, compared to 54% in wheat-after-wheat sequences.
- **Pulse Adaptation:** Faba beans are best suited for high-rainfall, heavy soils in WA, whereas chickpeas and lentils are more effective in low-rainfall Mediterranean environments.
