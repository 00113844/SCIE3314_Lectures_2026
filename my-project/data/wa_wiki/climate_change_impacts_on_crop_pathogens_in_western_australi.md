# Climate Change Impacts on Crop Pathogens in Western Australia

## Summary
The agricultural landscape of Western Australia is experiencing a drying trend and rising temperatures, which significantly alter the epidemiology of crop pathogens. Precision agriculture in this context relies heavily on predictive modeling to adjust sowing windows and management strategies to mitigate "loser" diseases while capitalizing on "winner" scenarios.

## Key Facts
- **Rainfall Trends:** Growing season rainfall in WA cropping areas has declined to 85–95% of historical levels, with a clear downward trend since 1967.
- **Temperature-Sensitive Resistance:** In canola, both quantitative and major gene resistance to blackleg are temperature-sensitive and may fail when temperatures exceed 13–15°C.
- **Rhizoctonia Dynamics:** Rhizoctonia bare patch is exacerbated by low rainfall, which reduces microbial competition in sandy soils, and by increased summer rains that support weed hosts.
- **Pathogen "Winners":** Field peas may benefit from climate shifts as pea black spot spore release windows move earlier, potentially expanding the viable sowing window by five weeks.
- **Stubble Decomposition:** Increased summer rainfall may accelerate the breakdown of crop residues, potentially allowing for tighter crop rotations (e.g., canola-wheat-canola).
- **Predictive Modeling:** Tools such as the Department of Agriculture’s blackleg sporacle model are critical for determining safe sowing dates with high temporal accuracy.

## Local Examples
- **Regional Blackleg Variation:** East Chapman (Northern WA) experiences a long pre-hazard period, whereas Mount Barker (Southern WA) faces early spore maturation and a prolonged hazard period.
- **Pea Black Spot Distribution:** Spore release occurs later in Geraldton (April–August) compared to Skadden, where release typically ends by late June.
- **Economic Impact:** Rhizoctonia bare patch costs the WA agricultural industry approximately $77 million annually.
