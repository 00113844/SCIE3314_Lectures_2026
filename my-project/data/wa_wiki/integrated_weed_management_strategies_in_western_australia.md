# Integrated Weed Management Strategies in Western Australia

## Summary
Integrated Weed Management (IWM) in Western Australian rain-fed cropping systems focuses on the strategic reduction and maintenance of weed seed banks to sustainable levels. By combining chemical controls, cultural competitiveness, and precision technology, growers aim to minimize the impact of both short-lived and persistent weed species on crop yields.

## Key Facts
- **Seed Bank Dynamics**: Most weeds have a seed bank life of 2–3 years; however, achieving near-zero levels for species like ryegrass requires at least three consecutive years of 100% seed set control.
- **Chemical Control Strategies**: A "double knock" approach—sequential application of a systemic herbicide (e.g., glyphosate) followed by a non-systemic herbicide (e.g., Sprayseed)—is used to kill survivors and mitigate herbicide resistance.
- **Precision Technology**: Systems such as "Weed Seeker" utilize cameras and red light reflectance to detect chlorophyll in fallow fields, enabling targeted spot-spraying and reducing chemical inputs.
- **Crop Competitiveness**: Cereal competitiveness varies by species, with barley being the most competitive due to strong tillering and early canopy shading, followed by wheat, while grain legumes are the least competitive.
- **Harvest Weed Seed Management (HWSM)**: To prevent the return of seeds to the soil, HWSM techniques such as narrow windrow burning, chaff carts, and the Harrington Seed Destructor (which physically destroys 90–95% of seeds) are employed.
- **Seed Purity**: Contaminated crop seed is a significant vector for weed introduction; surveys indicate that contaminated samples can introduce approximately one weed plant per square meter.

## Local Examples
- **Herbicide Products**: Use of Boxer Gold (prosulfacarb) and Sakura (pyroxasulfone) as pre-emergent options for WA cereal crops.
- **Regional Weed Pressure**: High prevalence of annual ryegrass, wild radish, and wild oats across WA cropping districts.
- **Industry Data**: A 2007–2008 survey revealed that 74% of farmer grain samples in the region were contaminated with an average of 14 weed seeds per kg.
