# Lupin Production and Management in Western Australia

## Summary
Lupins are critical nitrogen-fixing legumes integrated into Western Australian dryland farming systems to enhance soil fertility and provide disease breaks for cereals. Their ability to thrive in acidic sandy soils makes them a strategic choice for specific WA landscapes, though they face increasing economic competition from high-yielding canola varieties.

## Key Facts
- **Soil Adaptation:** Narrow-leaf lupins (*Lupinus angustifolius*) are the primary species grown in WA due to their high performance on acidic sandy soils compared to alkaline loams.
- **Nitrogen Fixation:** Symbiosis with rhizobia bacteria allows lupins to fix atmospheric nitrogen; inoculation is typically required every 5 to 7 years to maintain efficiency.
- **Establishment Targets:** Agronomic recommendations target a plant density of 45 plants per square meter, with seeding rates generally ranging from 80 to 120 kg/ha.
- **Nutritional Requirements:** While nitrogen requirements are minimal, phosphorus is essential. Trace elements such as molybdenum (critical for N-fixation) and manganese (to prevent seed splitting) are vital for yield and quality.
- **Weed Management:** Due to their broadleaf nature, lupins are bred for triazine tolerance to allow the use of pre-emergent herbicides like Simazine and Metribuzin.
- **Disease Pressure:** Key pathological challenges include Pleiochaeta root rot and Rhizoctonia bare patch, managed through rotation, stubble retention, and variety selection.

## Local Examples
- **University of Western Australia (UWA):** The site where John Angus domesticated the narrow-leaf lupin, developing the low-alkaloid, non-shattering 'Uni White' variety.
- **Sandplain Lupin (*Lupinus cosentinii*):** An early introduction (1850s) now largely relegated to roadsides due to high susceptibility to anthracnose.
- **Narrow-leaf Lupin:** The dominant commercial variety in WA, valued for high-protein grain used in sheep feed and commercial markets.
