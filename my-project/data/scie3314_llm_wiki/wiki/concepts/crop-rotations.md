# Crop rotations

A crop sequence is the order of crops; a phase is one crop within it; a rotation is the repeated sequence over time. Rotation is a management lever for disease, weeds, nitrogen, phosphorus availability, residue, water and operations.

## Benefits and limits

Break crops can suppress some cereal diseases, including take-all, and can reduce cereal cyst nematode. But not every disease is broken by a broadleaf crop: pathogens with broad host ranges, such as *Rhizoctonia solani*, need additional tactics.

Legumes may contribute nitrogen fixation and lower-C:N residue; some pulses can mobilise less available phosphorus through root exudates. These benefits coexist with water use and market risk, so a following-cereal benefit should not be attributed to nitrogen alone.

## Water and fallow

Broadleaf break crops can leave less residual water than a cereal in dry environments. Fallow can store water and mineralise nitrogen, but efficiency is often low because of evaporation and weeds; fine-textured soils generally store more than sands. The appropriate sequence balances water, disease, weed and nutrient effects against the crop forgone.

## Planning principles

Use diverse crop types, avoid repeated crop/crop-type phases where possible, plan for volunteers, use rotations to broaden weed-control tactics, and balance intensity with profit. Species identification is essential when rotation is used to manage nematodes.
