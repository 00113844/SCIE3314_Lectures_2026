## 1. Cropping systems and the decision frame

The unit frames agronomy as managing a whole farming system: biological production, profitability, risks, and longer-term resource condition must be considered together. The intended outcomes include understanding Australian agriculture in global food production, planning sustainable crop systems, managing pathogens and weeds, building agronomy packages for major WA grains, and using crop and weed models appropriately.

### The central challenge

WA farming systems need to remain profitable while responding to:

- changing climate patterns, including lower rainfall, drought, summer rainfall and false breaks;
- soil erosion and the need for conservation/no-till systems;
- rising fuel and input costs;
- herbicide-resistant weeds; and
- the need for rotations that are both agronomically effective and economic.

### A useful production model: G × E × M

Yield and quality result from the interaction of:

- **Genotype (G):** cultivar traits such as maturity, disease resistance, grain quality and herbicide tolerance.
- **Environment (E):** rainfall, temperature, soil type, stored water, disease pressure and other site conditions.
- **Management (M):** crop sequence, sowing time and density, nutrition, machinery, pest management and harvest decisions.

The interaction matters: an otherwise sound management choice can fail where the soil, season or variety do not support it.

## 2. Global food production

### Why world production matters

Food demand is driven by population, diets and income, while production is constrained by land, water, climate, technology and environmental limits. The lecture material highlights the need to address hunger and poverty while recognising that human activity is a major driver of change in the Earth system.

### Major grain and oilseed crops

The main global broadacre commodities discussed are wheat, rice, maize, soybeans, cottonseed, canola and lupins. Their importance differs by end use:

- **Wheat and rice** are central human food staples.
- **Maize and soybeans** are major feed, food and industrial crops.
- **Canola and sunflower** are important oilseeds.
- **Lupins** are a comparatively small global crop, with Australia an important producer.

### Geographic concentration creates supply risk

Production and exports are concentrated in a limited number of countries. This means conflict, weather shocks, trade restrictions or logistics failures in major producing regions can affect global availability and price. The lecture examples emphasise the global grain and oilseed significance of Ukraine and Russia, especially for wheat, barley, maize, sunflower and sunflower oil.

### Implication for Australian agronomy

Australian growers participate in globally exposed markets. Crop and variety choices therefore need to combine local agronomic fit with quality specifications, export market requirements, yield reliability and enterprise risk.

## 3. Farm economics and gross margins

### What a gross margin measures

An enterprise gross margin compares the contribution of enterprises to farm profit **before overhead costs**:

> **Gross margin = annual gross income − variable costs**

It is a comparison and planning tool, not an exact prediction. The lecture principle is that being *roughly right* is more useful than being precisely wrong with unsuitable assumptions.

### Costs usually excluded

Permanent labour, depreciation, accounting fees, rates and taxes, interest, general insurance, rent and family drawings are generally treated as overheads rather than crop-specific variable costs.

### Typical crop variable costs

Include the costs directly caused by producing that crop:

- land preparation and machinery operations;
- seed, fertiliser and sowing;
- weed, pest and disease control, including application costs;
- irrigation water, pumping, energy and licences where relevant;
- casual labour; and
- harvest, storage, freight and grain protection.

### How to use gross margins well

1. Use current local prices, yields and costs; preloaded guides are starting points only.
2. Compare profit with risk: calculate the break-even yield required to cover variable costs.
3. Test sensitive assumptions such as price, yield, fertiliser rate and freight.
4. Do not let a single-year gross margin displace rotation benefits or longer-term weed, disease, nutrition and soil-water effects.

## 4. Water, climate and crop modelling

### Rainfall deciles

A decile ranks a rainfall total against its historical distribution for the same place and period. The records are ordered from low to high and divided into ten equal groups:

- **Decile 1:** the lowest 10% of records;
- **Decile 5:** the median; and
- **Decile 10:** the highest 10% of records.

Deciles are a practical way to set plausible seasonal scenarios rather than treating the long-term mean as the only planning case.

### Setting a target yield from growing-season rainfall

Growing-season rainfall (GSR) is commonly considered from April to October. The lecture uses a simple water-limited planning approach:

> **Yield potential (kg/ha) = (GSR − evaporation allowance) × water-use efficiency**

Illustrative crop rules of thumb in the lectures are:

| Crop | Planning equation |
| --- | --- |
| Wheat | `(GSR − 110 mm) × 20 kg/ha/mm` |
| Barley | `(GSR − 90 mm) × 20 kg/ha/mm` |
| Pulses | `(GSR − 130 mm) × 15 kg/ha/mm` |
| Oilseeds | `(GSR − 110 mm) × 15 kg/ha/mm` |

A target may be set below this potential (the lecture example uses 75%) to reflect seasonal, soil and management risk. These are planning heuristics; local soil water, timing of rainfall and constraints can make actual yield very different.

### Yield Prophet and simulation modelling

Yield Prophet is presented as a decision-support approach that uses a crop model to simulate crop growth and help assess nitrogen decisions and yield prospects. The key input categories are:

- nearby weather-station data and seasonal rainfall;
- soil type, soil-water holding characteristics and starting soil water;
- soil analysis and nitrogen supply;
- crop variety, sowing date and seed rate;
- previous crop, stubble load and rotation history; and
- alternative management scenarios, particularly nitrogen rates.

Models are valuable for comparing scenarios and identifying likely risks. They do not eliminate uncertainty: input quality, the representation of soil constraints and future weather are limiting factors.

## 5. Paddock planning, soil and crop monitoring

### Keep useful records

Good decisions rely on a paddock history. Record soil analyses, rainfall, crop stresses, fertiliser treatments and rates, sowing details, plant-protection applications, machinery operations, fuel use and repairs. These records support diagnosis, resistance planning and later comparison of outcomes.

### Pre-season paddock questions

Before choosing the crop and agronomy package, assess:

- profitability and enterprise risk;
- soil capability and crop/variety fit;
- prior crops, stubble, disease, herbicide carryover and residual nitrogen;
- herbicide-use history and resistance risk;
- fertility and subsoil constraints; and
- disease and nematode risk, including pre-season DNA-based testing where appropriate.

### Soil water and soil constraints

At field capacity, clay soils normally hold more water than sands, but the fraction available to plants depends on the gap between field capacity and permanent wilting point. Soil water can be reported gravimetrically or volumetrically; volumetric water content is calculated from gravimetric water content and bulk density.

Important subsoil constraints and the lecture responses include:

| Constraint | Possible response |
| --- | --- |
| Compaction | Ripping and controlled-traffic farming |
| Sodicity | Gypsum where appropriate |
| Salinity | Use more tolerant crop options |
| Low or high pH | Lime or choose a crop tolerant of the condition |
| Boron toxicity | Tolerant crops; zinc nutrition may help in some cases |
| Water repellence | Clay incorporation or surface wetting agents |

### Soil sampling principles

- Avoid field margins and unrepresentative areas.
- Sample separately by soil type or management zone where possible.
- Record GPS locations so sites can be revisited.
- Know the soil texture and sampling depth.
- Consider the previous crop row and inter-row: fertiliser bands can make results differ sharply.

### Interpreting soil tests

Interpret a test in its context: crop, soil type, pH, seasonal conditions, sampling depth and chosen test all matter.

- **pH (CaCl₂):** phosphorus availability is reduced at strongly acid and strongly alkaline pH. The lecture guide is to maintain surface pH around 5.0–5.5 and to aim for 5.5 where practical; use a layered liming plan rather than relying only on the surface sample.
- **Phosphorus:** WA commonly uses bicarbonate/Colwell-P, but other methods exist. PBI (phosphorus buffering index) changes how a P test should be interpreted: high-PBI soil binds P more strongly, while low-PBI soils are more affected by pH and rainfall around seeding. DGT-P provides another measure of P supply.
- **Nitrogen, sulphur, potassium and organic carbon:** assess quantity, placement, crop demand, soil storage and loss pathways, rather than using a generic target alone.
- **Salinity and cations:** electrical conductivity, CEC and exchangeable Na, Ca, Mg and K help identify salinity, sodicity and nutrient constraints.
- **Trace elements:** plant tissue testing is often needed to confirm suspected copper or zinc deficiency because soil availability changes strongly with pH and sorption.

### Stubble, seed and establishment

- Retained stubble protects soil: the lecture guide is about 1.5–2 t/ha of stubble and 75–85% cover to minimise erosion.
- Burning can lose large proportions of stubble nitrogen and a portion of sulphur, phosphorus and potassium.
- Check seed for seed-borne disease and calculate seed rate from target plants/m², thousand-seed weight and expected establishment.
- Match sowing time to crop and flowering window: early sowing may suit canola and lupins, while disease risk can make some pulses later-sown crops. In wheat, flowering outside the optimal period can impose rapid yield losses.

## 6. Diagnosing crop problems

### A disciplined diagnosis process

Above-ground symptoms are rarely proof of a single cause. Begin with a whole-paddock assessment and move from field pattern to plant symptoms, roots, records and tests.

1. Do not jump to conclusions.
2. Compare plants from poor and good areas.
3. Map the pattern: patchy, wavy, headland, low-lying, windrow or soil-type patterns are clues.
4. Inspect the entire plant, especially roots.
5. Check management and weather records.
6. Verify with soil, tissue, seed, grain or pathogen tests where needed.
7. Allow for multiple interacting causes.

### Symptom vocabulary

Useful visible terms include lesions, pustules, blotches, necrosis, chlorosis/colour change and abnormal growth. Ask whether symptoms occur predominantly on old leaves, new leaves, roots, or neither; this can help distinguish mobile-nutrient, immobile-nutrient, disease and physical causes.

### Verification tools

Use plant nutrient tests, grain tests, laboratory diagnosis, soil test reports and DNA-based tests such as Predicta B in combination with field evidence. Paddock history and field clues are not substitutes for tests, but they make testing more targeted and interpretable.

### Plant tissue sampling

For cereals, sample around tillering when there is still time to correct some deficiencies. Sample whole shoots when plants are small; otherwise use the youngest fully expanded leaf (YFEL), especially for relatively immobile trace elements. The lecture uses youngest mature blade (YMB) benchmarks to distinguish deficient from adequate nutrient concentrations in wheat and barley.

For canola, sampling is commonly at rosette stage using a middle, young fully developed leaf. Nutrient concentrations must be interpreted by crop, growth stage, tissue and laboratory method; a number on its own is not a diagnosis.

## 7. Crop rotations

### Terms

- **Crop sequence:** the order of crops, for example wheat → lupin → canola.
- **Phase:** one crop within the sequence, such as the wheat phase.
- **Cycle:** a repeat of the full sequence.
- **Rotation:** the repeated crop sequence over time.

### Why rotations work

Rotations can change disease, weed, nitrogen, phosphorus availability, soil water, crop residue and operational timing. A break crop may improve the following wheat crop for several reasons at once; the benefit should not automatically be attributed to nitrogen alone.

### Disease and nematodes

Rotation is a main strategy for several cereal diseases, including take-all, and can reduce cereal cyst nematode. It is not universal: some pathogens, such as *Rhizoctonia solani*, have broad host ranges, so a broadleaf break crop alone may not provide adequate control.

### Weeds and resistance

Crop diversity gives access to different herbicides, sowing times, crop competition and harvest tactics. It is a core part of integrated weed management because it reduces repeated selection for the same resistant weed types.

### Nutrition and soil structure

Legume phases may increase nitrogen supply through rhizobial fixation and can leave lower-C:N residues that immobilise less nitrogen during breakdown. Some pulses can mobilise less available phosphorus forms through root exudates. Deep tap-rooted break crops can also create macropores and improve rooting pathways, although the net benefit depends on crop growth and soil conditions.

### Water and fallow

Broadleaf break crops can leave less residual water than a cereal in semi-arid environments. Fallow can store water and increase mineral nitrogen, but its efficiency is often low because of evaporation and weed use; weed control is therefore critical. Fine-textured soils generally store fallow rainfall more effectively than sands. The best sequence balances water, disease, weed and nitrogen effects against the profitability of the crop forgone.

### Planning principles

- Prefer a multi-year, diverse rotation rather than a sequence selected only for the next crop.
- Where feasible, leave at least a two-season interval before repeating a crop or crop type; some diseases need longer.
- Include both broadleaf and grass crops and, where possible, differing maturity and growing-season types.
- Plan for volunteers from the previous crop.
- Treat chemical fallow as one tool, not a complete substitute for a biologically diverse rotation.
- Balance diversity and intensity with whole-farm profitability.

## 8. Barley agronomy and grain quality

### Biology and market classes

Domesticated barley is *Hordeum vulgare*. Two-row types have one fertile floret per node; six-row types have three. Grain is marketed as malt or feed. Malt acceptance normally requires an accredited variety and compliance with specifications for moisture, protein, screenings, test weight, germination, variety purity, colour and skinning.

### The malt-versus-feed decision

Choose a malting variety only after considering:

- the likely malt premium;
- yield relative to feed options;
- disease and agronomic fit; and
- the probability of meeting malt grade.

The best-yielding crop is not always the best enterprise outcome if quality downgrading is likely.

### Nitrogen, yield and protein

Cereals accumulate most nitrogen before flowering, particularly during stem elongation when biomass and leaf area increase quickly. Later, nitrogen is remobilised to the grain, influencing protein. In barley, the nitrogen rate and site are primary controls of grain protein; variety and split timing are secondary tools.

For malt barley, both low and high grain protein are undesirable. High nitrogen may lift protein but can also create a quality mismatch or increase the risk of small grain if spring water is insufficient. Match nitrogen supply to realistic yield and quality targets, considering soil organic carbon, rotation history, soil test results and seasonal outlook. Yield Prophet can be used to compare the nitrogen rate needed for a target protein range.

### Screenings and grain plumpness

Screenings measure the proportion of small grain passing through a specified screen and are a proxy for plumpness and starch. They are affected by:

- available water during grain fill;
- nitrogen supply relative to water;
- soil type and rotation;
- disease and insect damage;
- variety and grain shape; and
- sowing time, seed rate and weeds.

The lecture analogy is useful: nitrogen is the accelerator and water is the fuel tank. Extra N cannot produce plump grain without adequate water.

### Grain colour and disease

Light showers or high humidity can cause yellowing without fungal infection. Heavy rain near maturity can cause greying associated with moulds and fungi. Grain discolouration can restrict suitability for pale-beer markets and may signal storage or malting problems.

### Practical malting-barley package

- Select a variety with market acceptance, disease resistance and local fit.
- Prefer soil types that can carry water into spring.
- Sow as early as is appropriate for the variety and flowering target.
- Match N to yield and protein targets; do not pursue protein independently of water supply.
- Establish an adequate crop (lecture guide: about 120–150 plants/m²).
- Use non-legume rotations where they help manage protein and disease risk.
- Use seed dressing, monitor foliar disease, protect grain fill from aphids and control weeds early with integrated weed management.

## 9. Lupin production

### Role and adaptation

Lupins are Fabaceae legumes with a valuable place in WA sandplain systems. Narrow-leafed lupin (*Lupinus angustifolius*) is the main commercial type in WA and is well adapted to acid, infertile sands. Albus and yellow lupins have different adaptation and disease profiles; crop choice must match soil pH, waterlogging risk, disease pressure and market.

Lupins can be a cash crop or on-farm protein feed and provide a disease break, alternative weed-control options, nitrogen fixation, useful residue and operational spread. Constraints include weed-control cost, disease, a relatively small market and competition with soybean protein.

### Establishment and canopy

- Target approximately **40–45 plants/m²** for crop competition, yield and harvestability.
- Sow about **3–5 cm** deep. Very deep sowing weakens and delays emergence; shallow or deep placement can also change root-disease risk.
- Inoculate with the appropriate rhizobia. The lecture notes more frequent inoculation where pH is above 6.5 and less frequent use on acidic soils, but local inoculant and seed-treatment guidance should be followed.
- Wider rows can be workable in warm, dry environments, but row spacing should be evaluated against crop competition, machinery and moisture conditions.

### Nutrition

Lupins obtain much of their nitrogen from symbiotic fixation but may need a small starter N supply in some situations. They also need adequate phosphorus, potassium and trace elements. Manganese, molybdenum, copper and zinc are important examples; liming may change Mn availability, while Mo is needed for effective nitrogen fixation.

### Weed management

Weeds are often more difficult and expensive to control in lupins. The crop has non-GM tolerance to triazines, allowing useful pre- and post-emergent options, but this must be integrated with crop competition, residual herbicides, rotation, resistance status and label constraints. Crop topping can reduce weed seed set in suitable situations.

### Disease management

Key risks in the lectures are:

- **Pleiochaeta root rot:** soil-borne; manage with rotation and stubble retained on the surface to reduce soil splash.
- **Rhizoctonia bare patch and hypocotyl rot:** broad host range limits the value of rotation alone; soil disturbance at seeding can disrupt hyphae.
- **Eradu patch:** manage with crop type and rotation; susceptibility differs among lupin types.
- **Anthracnose:** seed-borne and serious; use certified seed and suitable seed treatment. It affects lupins but not other broadacre crops.

### Rotation benefits and limits

Lupins can leave nitrogen and improve the following cereal crop, but the amount of residue nitrogen that becomes available is uncertain and can be reduced by grazing, erosion and nitrate leaching. Treat the N benefit as a contribution to be measured or budgeted, not a guaranteed fertiliser replacement.

## 10. Weed and herbicide management

### Why herbicides remain important

Herbicides enable no-till systems and early sowing by replacing frequent soil cultivation. They are essential tools in Australian rainfed broadacre cropping, but they are not a stand-alone strategy because resistance, crop injury, residues, off-target effects and poor timing can all erode their value.

### Core classifications

| Classification | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Selective | Controls some species while being safe in the labelled crop when used correctly. |
| Non-selective | Broad-spectrum activity; often used before crop emergence or in fallow. |
| Pre-plant | Applied before seeding. |
| Pre-emergent / soil-applied | Controls germinating weeds through soil uptake, typically around seeding. |
| Post-emergent | Applied to foliage after weed emergence; may or may not also have residual soil activity. |

### Soil-applied herbicides

In no-till systems, pre-emergent herbicides are generally sprayed close to seeding and incorporated by rainfall or light soil throw from the seeder. They are most active on very young germinating weeds. Soil water is critical for uptake and activity.

Performance and crop safety depend on water solubility, soil binding, degradation, volatility, soil type, placement and the crop’s ability to avoid the treated zone. Positional safety through incorporation by sowing is a central crop-safety concept.

### Foliar herbicides

Leaf uptake is best on young, actively growing and unstressed plants. It is affected by leaf angle, hairs, wax, droplet retention, spray-solution surface tension and weather. Systemic herbicides can move through:

- the **apoplast** (water-filled cell-wall and xylem pathway; mainly upward); or
- the **symplast** (living cells and phloem; movement can occur from source to sink, including downward).

### Mode of action and resistance

Repeated use of a single mode of action selects resistant weeds. Rotate and mix effective modes of action, use non-herbicide tactics and keep records of use. Groups highlighted in the lecture include ACCase (Group 1/A), ALS (Group 2/B), dinitroanilines (Group 3/D), auxin mimics (Group 4/I), PSII inhibitors/triazines (Group 5/C), EPSPS inhibitors/glyphosate (Group 9/M) and pyridiniums such as paraquat and diquat (Group 22/L).

Use the current Australian mode-of-action classification and the product label: group numbering, approvals and stewardship requirements can change.

## 11. Broadacre spray application

### The objective

Effective spraying applies the specified product rate uniformly to the target, while minimising drift, off-target impacts, crop damage, waste and operator exposure. Safety and legal requirements come first.

### Droplet size is a trade-off

Droplet size is commonly described by volume median diameter (VMD): half of the spray volume is in smaller droplets and half in larger droplets. Broad lecture categories are:

| Category | Approximate VMD | Main implication |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Coarse | >300 µm | Lower drift; less coverage and retention; often suits soil-active herbicides. |
| Medium | 200–300 µm | Useful balance of coverage, penetration and drift for many foliar applications. |
| Fine | 100–200 µm | High coverage but higher drift risk. |

Select the label-required spray quality; never choose a finer spray simply to increase apparent coverage.

### Nozzle and pressure selection

Hydraulic nozzles are standard in broadacre cropping and produce a distribution of droplet sizes. Common designs include flat-fan, deflector, full-cone, hollow-cone, pre-orifice/reduced-drift and air-induction nozzles. Air-induction and pre-orifice designs can produce coarser droplets and reduce drift potential.

Nozzle flow is proportional to the square root of pressure. Therefore, **doubling flow requires roughly four times the pressure**. Raising pressure to achieve a higher application rate also tends to make droplets finer and can increase drift; it is not an efficient general substitute for choosing the correct nozzle size.

Boom height must match fan angle and nozzle spacing to maintain overlap without increasing drift exposure. Check the manufacturer chart rather than relying on a generic setting.

### Suitable spray conditions

The lecture guide is to spray below about 28°C with high humidity, Delta T below about 8, and wind roughly 3–15 km/h. Temperature inversions are a major risk because fine droplets can remain suspended and move long distances. Always apply the current product label and local restrictions.

### Calibration

Measure actual nozzle output at the intended pressure, vehicle speed under paddock conditions and nozzle spacing. For a boom sprayer:

> **Application rate (L/ha) = total sprayer output (L/min) × 600 ÷ [speed (km/h) × swath width (m)]**

For a product rate, calculate the product per tank from the desired amount per hectare, tank volume and calibrated application volume. Recalibrate after changing nozzles, pressure, speed, spray mix or equipment condition; check every nozzle for wear and variation.

### Rate-control technologies

Pressure-based systems change pressure with speed but can alter droplet size and pattern. Multi-nozzle, dual-boom and variable-rate nozzles broaden the usable flow range. Pulse-width modulation (PWM) controls flow by rapidly opening and closing individual nozzles, allowing rate changes across a wider speed range while holding pressure and droplet size more stable. It can also support individual-nozzle control and turn compensation, but it requires appropriate nozzle selection and operator understanding.

### Adjuvants

Adjuvants modify the tank mix, spray behaviour, target retention or uptake. Examples include surfactants, oils, drift retardants, water conditioners and buffers. They can improve or reduce performance depending on the product and target—glyphosate and atrazine, for example, do not necessarily respond the same way to oils. Follow the label; correct nozzle selection and pressure are usually the first drift-management tools.

## 12. Nematodes in cereal cropping

### The management mindset

Plant-parasitic nematodes are small, mostly transparent root parasites that use a stylet to puncture cells. In broadacre systems, the realistic objective is **management rather than eradication**. Effective management requires species identification and quantification, knowledge of host range and rotation effects, and an understanding that above-ground symptoms are non-specific and patchy.

### Root lesion nematodes (RLN)

*Pratylenchus* species are migratory endoparasites that damage lateral and feeder roots. Typical signs are shallow discoloured roots, stunting, chlorosis and uneven patches, but these signs cannot identify the species. RLN can survive drying through anhydrobiosis and can complete multiple generations in a season.

Management relies on diagnosis, species-specific rotation, weed control, resistant or tolerant crops/varieties, nutrition and suitable sowing decisions. Chemical and tillage solutions are limited or unreliable in the lecture framework.

### Species identification is essential

Different RLN species can cause identical symptoms but have different host ranges. A rotation that lowers *P. neglectus*, such as some wheat–lupin–field pea sequences, may not manage *P. penetrans*. Mixed populations further complicate decisions. Test before using a crop break as a nematode-control action.

### Cereal cyst nematode (CCN)

CCN, *Heterodera avenae*, is restricted to grasses such as cereals and grass weeds. It can cause yellowing, stunting, reduced tillering, shallow roots and distinctive knotting/cysts on cereal roots. It is often easier to diagnose than RLN because root symptoms are more distinctive.

Manage CCN with non-host crops, resistant and/or tolerant cereal cultivars, grass-weed control and nutrition. If CCN is present, choose a resistant variety or non-host crop in the following season.

### Resistance versus tolerance

- **Tolerance:** the current crop maintains yield despite nematode attack.
- **Resistance:** the crop suppresses nematode reproduction and reduces future populations.

A variety can be tolerant but susceptible, yielding acceptably while multiplying the pest. Good system management needs both the current-crop yield response and the population effect considered.

## 13. Precision agriculture

### Definition and purpose

Precision agriculture is a management strategy that gathers, processes and analyses temporal, spatial and plant/animal data, combining it with other information to improve resource-use efficiency, productivity, quality, profitability and sustainability. Its core principle is to account for variation rather than manage every part of a paddock as if it were identical.

### When site-specific management has potential

The opportunity is greatest where variation is both:

- **large enough** to justify a different decision; and
- **spatially coherent and repeatable**, rather than random noise.

A field averaging 2.5 t/ha because it has stable 1 and 4 t/ha zones is more promising for zone management than one with the same average but scattered, unexplained variation.

### Measuring variation

Useful spatial layers include:

- calibrated yield-monitor data;
- crop biomass and vegetation indices from sensors or satellites;
- weed, insect and disease maps;
- soil apparent electrical conductivity (ECa), soil depth and texture;
- elevation, slope and drainage information; and
- targeted soil samples.

Plants reflect red, green and near-infrared radiation differently according to chlorophyll, structure and biomass. Vegetation indices make crop variation easier to see than true-colour imagery alone, but they do not diagnose the cause of variation by themselves.

### From maps to actions

Spatial layers are put on a common grid, interpreted as management zones and tested against ground truth. Some decisions can follow directly from production maps, such as nutrient replacement. For example, the lecture uses wheat grain phosphorus removal of roughly 4 kg P/t as the basis for a P-replacement map. Other decisions require investigation first: a low-yield zone could be due to shallow soil, salinity, disease, waterlogging, weeds or an operational issue, each requiring a different response.

### Variable-rate technology (VRT)

VRT can vary fertiliser, lime, gypsum, herbicide type/rate and other inputs by management zone or in real time. It is data-intensive and should be adopted when the likely value of a better decision exceeds the cost of data collection, analysis, equipment and implementation. A well-calibrated uniform-rate practice can be better than an unvalidated variable-rate map.

## 14. An integrated paddock workflow

Use the topics above as one connected cycle rather than separate checklists.

1. **Review the history.** Gather past crops, weeds, herbicides, disease/nematode tests, yields, soil maps, rainfall and gross margins.
2. **Define constraints and opportunity.** Delineate soil types or management zones; test for fertility, pH, salinity, soil water and pathogens where justified.
3. **Choose a rotation position.** Select the crop and cultivar for disease, weed, nitrogen, soil-water and market consequences, not only this season’s price.
4. **Set yield and quality targets.** Use deciles, stored water, soil capability and a model or water-limited calculation to set realistic targets and nitrogen scenarios.
5. **Build the agronomy package.** Specify sowing time, seed rate, crop nutrition, stubble handling, weed strategy, disease plan and harvest-quality risks.
6. **Apply inputs accurately.** Calibrate the sprayer and seeder; use the label, suitable spray quality, weather conditions and resistance stewardship.
7. **Monitor and diagnose.** Walk the paddock, compare good and poor zones, inspect roots, use targeted diagnostics and adapt where an in-season response is still worthwhile.
8. **Harvest, evaluate and learn.** Record yield, quality, costs, herbicide performance and spatial patterns. Update the gross margin, rotation plan and any precision-agriculture zones for the next season.
