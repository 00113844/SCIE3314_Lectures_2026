## Teaching Notes Open by situating Australian agriculture within global food production and resolving an apparent paradox: Australia produces only a small share of the world's grain (on the order of 3–4% of global wheat) yet is one of the world's most important grain exporters, typically ranking among the top few wheat exporters and dominating some markets such as malting barley and certain pulses. The explanation is the extraordinary export orientation of the system: with a small domestic population, Australia consumes little of what it grows and exports the majority — the reverse of large producers like China, India, the United States, or the EU, which grow far more but consume most of it domestically. This makes Australia a "swing supplier" whose exportable surplus matters disproportionately to world trade and to food-importing nations in Asia, the Middle East, and Africa. ## Teaching Notes Introduce the Mediterranean-type climate as the defining environmental context for southern and western Australian grain production, and one of only five such regions in the world (the Mediterranean basin, California, central Chile, the Western Cape of South Africa, and southern and south-western Australia). ## Teaching Notes Establish the biophysical foundation of WA agriculture: some of the oldest, most weathered, and most infertile soils on earth, farmed for little more than a century. Explain that the south-west of WA has not been geologically rejuvenated by recent glaciation, volcanism, or mountain building, so its soils are deeply weathered, low in inherent phosphorus and trace elements, low in organic matter, often sandy and water-repellent, and frequently underlain by acidic, sodic, or compacted subsoils. Contrast this with the young, fertile soils of Europe or North America. The consequence is that WA agriculture has always depended on correcting fundamental deficiencies: the discovery in the early twentieth century that trace elements (copper, zinc, molybdenum) and phosphorus and, later, nitrogen and potassium were limiting transformed vast tracts of "useless" land into productive farmland. ## Teaching Notes Trace the historical development of the classic WA and southern Australian wheat-sheep mixed farming system that dominated through most of the twentieth century. Close Part 2 by confronting the sustainability challenge inherent in intensive cropping of a fragile, ancient landscape — a theme that recurs throughout the unit.

## Teaching Notes Close Part 2 by confronting the sustainability challenge inherent in intensive cropping of a fragile, ancient landscape — a theme that recurs throughout the unit. Enumerate the major resource-degradation processes that WA farming systems must manage to remain productive: soil acidification driven by nitrogen cycling and product removal (the lime story from the soils lecture); wind and water erosion on light soils (managed by conservation agriculture); dryland salinity caused by rising water tables after the clearing of deep-rooted native vegetation; soil structural decline, compaction, and water repellence; herbicide resistance from over-reliance on chemical weed control; and declining soil organic carbon. Frame sustainable production not as a constraint opposed to profit but as the maintenance of the natural capital on which future profit depends — degrading the soil resource borrows yield from the future.
