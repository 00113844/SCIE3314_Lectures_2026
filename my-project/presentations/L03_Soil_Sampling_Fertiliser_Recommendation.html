## Teaching Notes Explain that the largest source of error in soil testing is not the laboratory analysis but the sampling. A soil test measures a few hundred grams of soil taken to represent tens of hectares, so how those cores are taken determines whether the result means anything. Cover the principles of good sampling. Depth: nutrients and pH stratify strongly, especially under no-tillage where lime and phosphorus concentrate near the surface, so sampling must be to a defined, consistent depth (commonly 0–10 cm) and, critically for acidity, also at depth (10–20 and 20–30 cm) to detect subsurface acidity that a surface sample alone would miss. Consistency and timing: sample the same zones at the same time of year to make results comparable between years. Avoiding artefacts: keep clear of fertiliser bands, stubble heaps, gateways, and old fence lines. ## Teaching Notes Explain how a soil-test number becomes a fertiliser recommendation, and why this rests on decades of field calibration. A critical value is the soil-test level above which a crop is very unlikely to respond economically to added nutrient — for example, a Colwell P critical value that varies with PBI. These critical values are established by conducting many nutrient-rate response trials across seasons and soils, plotting relative yield against soil-test level, and identifying the level at which yield reaches ~90–95% of maximum. Introduce the diminishing-returns shape of the yield-response curve: the first units of a limiting nutrient produce large yield gains, but response flattens as the deficiency is removed, so the economically optimal rate is well below the yield-maximising rate and depends on the ratio of grain price to fertiliser cost. This is where the agronomy meets the economics developed in the farm financial lecture: the recommendation is not "the rate that maximises yield" but "the rate that maximises expected profit given price, cost, and seasonal risk". ## Teaching Notes Explain the central agronomic reason acidity matters: aluminium toxicity. In soil, aluminium is locked in harmless mineral forms at neutral pH, but as pHCa falls below approximately 4.5–4.8 it dissolves into the soil solution as toxic aluminium ions. These ions poison root tips, stunting and thickening roots and drastically reducing their capacity to explore the soil for water and nutrients. The consequence is severe in a Mediterranean environment: a crop with a restricted, aluminium-pruned root system cannot access subsoil moisture during the terminal drought, so acidity translates directly into reduced water use, poor nutrient uptake, and yield loss even when surface conditions look adequate. Note the interacting factors — manganese toxicity and molybdenum and phosphorus availability also worsen at low pH — and the strong species and variety differences in aluminium tolerance (barley is highly sensitive; some wheat and triticale, and lupins, tolerate more), which becomes a management lever. ## Teaching Notes Introduce agricultural lime (calcium carbonate) as the primary and only economic long-term treatment for soil acidity. ## Teaching Notes Frame liming as a capital investment in the soil resource rather than an annual input, and work through why its economics are genuinely different from those of fertiliser. Lime is relatively cheap per tonne but bulky and costly to cart and spread, and its benefit accrues slowly over many years as pH rises and — especially — as it moves and neutralises the subsurface. This creates a long payback period and a temptation to defer liming when cash is tight, which is precisely the wrong response because acidification continues in the meantime and the cost of recovery grows. Present the long-term economic case: modelling and long-term trials consistently show that maintaining pH above critical thresholds through regular liming delivers a strongly positive net present value over a 10–20 year horizon, that the cost of remediating badly acidified subsoil greatly exceeds the cost of preventing it, and that liming to prevent acidity is one of the highest-returning long-term investments available to a grower. Discuss the practical decisions this frames: maintenance liming rate to hold pH steady versus higher ameliorative rates to recover degraded paddocks, and the case for incorporation where subsurface acidity is advanced. ## Teaching Notes Consolidate the acidity story into a practical interpretation workflow that students can apply. Read pHCa at each sampled depth: surface pH above ~5.5 but subsurface below ~4.8 signals the classic hidden subsurface-acidity problem requiring attention despite a healthy-looking surface. Cross-check with exchangeable aluminium: rising aluminium confirms the acidity is biologically active and yield-limiting. ## Teaching Notes Turn from soil chemistry to two establishment decisions that strongly influence yield and are made at seeding: plant density and sowing time. Explain the biology of plant density. ## Teaching Notes Present sowing date as arguably the most powerful yield lever a WA grower controls, and explain the biology behind the strong, consistent yield penalty for late sowing. ## Teaching Notes Develop the central establishment trade-off that ties density, sowing time, variety, and frost together. Because yield declines with delayed sowing, growers want to sow as early as possible; but sowing early with a quick-developing variety pushes flowering into the frost window, and a single radiation frost at flowering can sterilise a cereal crop and destroy yield. The resolution is to match variety phenology to sowing date so that flowering, regardless of when sowing occurs, lands in the safe window: slower-developing (winter or long-season) varieties can be sown early because they delay flowering, while quick (spring) varieties are reserved for later sowing. Introduce the modern trend toward earlier sowing enabled by longer-season varieties, dry sowing (sowing into dry soil before the break to be ready for the first rain), and better seeding technology — collectively lifting whole-farm yield by getting more of the crop into the ground early. Note the interaction with the broader system: earlier sowing demands earlier weed control decisions, dry-sowing changes herbicide strategy, and the whole approach depends on the machinery capacity and logistics developed in later lectures. ## Teaching Notes Synthesise the lecture into the integrated decision-making a grower and agronomist undertake before each season, showing how soil testing, amelioration, nutrition, and establishment fit into one plan. Start from the paddock history and soil tests: identify constraints (acidity and aluminium, phosphorus and potassium status, nitrogen supply from mineralisation and the previous legume, trace elements). Decide amelioration: lime rate and whether incorporation is needed, planned as a multi-year program. Set the nutrition program: phosphorus and potassium to correct deficiency and replace removal, sulphur and trace elements as needed, and a nitrogen strategy matched to the yield potential the season is expected to allow — with in-season flexibility to top up if the season develops well. Emphasise that every one of these decisions is made under uncertainty about the season ahead, that they interact (a legume in the rotation changes the nitrogen plan; an acidity constraint changes the variety choice), and that good records and soil monitoring make each year's plan better than the last. This integrated pre-season planning is the practical embodiment of the systems thinking the unit teaches. Philosophical Approaches to Fertilizer Recommendations Three main approaches are discussed: build-up and maintenance, percent sufficiency (crop nutrient requirement), and basic cation saturation ratios. These approaches differ in how they interpret soil test indices and decide when and how much fertilizer to apply, especially regarding whether to apply zero fertilizer at high soil nutrient levels. Build-up and Maintenance Approach Fertilizer is added to build soil nutrient levels to a desired high index and then maintained by replacing nutrients removed by crops. Relies heavily on accurate crop nutrient removal values and often results in annual fertilizer applications. May not be suitable for rented land or soils prone to erosion; environmental and economic considerations are important. Percent Sufficiency (Crop Nutrient Requirement) Approach Fertilizes the crop by adding nutrients only when soil test levels fall below a critical threshold. Above this threshold, no fertilizer is recommended, relying on well-calibrated soil tests. Favored by land grant universities and considered more conservative and environmentally friendly. Basic Cation Saturation Ratios Approach Focuses on maintaining ideal ratios of cations (calcium, magnesium, potassium) on soil exchange sites. Often difficult to manage, especially in sandy soils with low cation exchange capacity. Research shows little yield benefit compared to other approaches, and plants can tolerate a wide range of cation ratios. Comparative Research and Practical Considerations Studies show no significant yield differences among these approaches but differences in fertilizer costs. Increasing fertilizer costs and environmental concerns are shifting recommendations toward the percent sufficiency approach. Yield goals used for nitrogen recommendations must be realistic to avoid over-fertilization.
