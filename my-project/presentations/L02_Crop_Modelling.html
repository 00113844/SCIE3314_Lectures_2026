What Is a Model, and Why Do Agronomists Use Them?

  • Motivate modelling in agriculture with the practical reality that field experiments are slow, expensive.

Takeaway

a simplified representation of a real system, built to help us understand it, predict its behaviour, or make decisions about it.

## Teaching Notes Open by defining a model in the broadest sense: a simplified representation of a real system, built to help us understand it, predict its behaviour, or make decisions about it. Emphasise that a model is deliberately incomplete — it captures the processes that matter for the question at hand and discards the rest. Motivate modelling in agriculture with the practical reality that field experiments are slow, expensive, and confounded by the single season and site in which they are run: a nitrogen-rate trial answers the question only for that paddock in that year's weather. A model, once built and tested, lets us ask "what if" across thousands of combinations of variety, sowing date, nitrogen rate, soil, and season in minutes, and lets us extrapolate — carefully — to seasons and sites we have never trialled. Introduce the three enduring reasons to model: prediction (what will happen if I do X?), understanding (writing the equations forces explicit, testable hypotheses about how a process works), and integration (only a model can track soil, water, nitrogen, weather, and crop physiology interacting simultaneously). ## Teaching Notes Anchor the lecture in George Box's aphorism — "all models are wrong, but some are useful" — and use it to establish the mature, critical attitude the unit expects toward every model and sensor. Explain what "wrong" means: every model omits processes, approximates others, and relies on parameters measured elsewhere that may not represent the target system. This is not a failure but a design choice. The right question is never "is this model correct?" but "is this model fit for the purpose I am using it for?" A simple rule-of-thumb yield calculator may be perfectly adequate for a quick paddock benchmark yet hopelessly inadequate for optimising a split nitrogen application on a waterlogging-prone duplex soil. Introduce the companion idea of appropriate complexity: adding detail to a model only improves it if the data and understanding exist to parameterise that detail; beyond a point, extra complexity adds uncertainty faster than it adds accuracy. Modelling and Experimentation: Partners, Not Rivals

  • In reality the two are complementary and mutually dependent.

Takeaway

Correct the common misconception that models replace field experiments.

## Teaching Notes Correct the common misconception that models replace field experiments. In reality the two are complementary and mutually dependent. Experiments generate the observations needed to build, calibrate, and validate models; models, in turn, extend the reach of experiments by interpolating between treatments, extrapolating to unsampled seasons and sites, and identifying which new experiments would be most informative. Describe the virtuous cycle: field data reveal a pattern; a model formalises a hypothesis about the mechanism; the model predicts behaviour in untested conditions; a new experiment tests that prediction; disagreement exposes a flaw in the model and prompts refinement. Give the concrete example that underpins WA nitrogen research — long-term trials at a handful of sites provide the calibration data, and APSIM then generalises those findings across the full range of soils, seasons, and management the grainbelt actually experiences, something no feasible number of trials could sample. ## Teaching Notes Introduce the first and most important axis for classifying models. A descriptive (empirical) model captures a statistical relationship observed in data without representing the underlying mechanism — for example, a regression of yield on growing-season rainfall, which fits the data it was built from but says nothing about why the relationship holds. An explanatory (mechanistic, process-based) model represents the actual biological and physical processes — light interception, photosynthesis, water uptake, nitrogen dynamics — so that the outcome emerges from the interaction of those processes. Contrast their strengths: empirical models are simple, transparent, and accurate within the range of data used to build them, but dangerous when extrapolated beyond that range; mechanistic models are harder to build and parameterise but can be applied, with care, to novel conditions (new varieties, future climates) because they encode causation rather than correlation. Part 1 · Core idea

Static versus Dynamic Models

  • A static model produces an output from a set of inputs without any representation of time — for example, a fertiliser recommendation calculated from a soil test.
  • A dynamic model tracks the evolution of state variables (soil water, crop biomass, leaf area, soil nitrogen) step by step, usually on a daily time step.
  • Crop growth models are dynamic by necessity, because the value of a resource on any given day depends on the entire history that preceded it: today's crop growth depends on how much water.
Takeaway

A static model produces an output from a set of inputs without any representation of time — for example, a fertiliser recommendation calculated from a soil test.

## Teaching Notes Explain the distinction between models that represent a system at a single point in time and those that simulate its change through time. A static model produces an output from a set of inputs without any representation of time — for example, a fertiliser recommendation calculated from a soil test. A dynamic model tracks the evolution of state variables (soil water, crop biomass, leaf area, soil nitrogen) step by step, usually on a daily time step, with the state at the end of each day becoming the starting point for the next. Crop growth models are dynamic by necessity, because the value of a resource on any given day depends on the entire history that preceded it: today's crop growth depends on how much water was used yesterday, which depended on how big the canopy had grown, which depended on the nitrogen taken up weeks earlier. Deterministic versus Stochastic Models

  • Distinguish models that produce a single definite output from a given set of inputs (deterministic) from those that incorporate randomness and produce a distribution of possible outputs.
  • Most crop models, including APSIM, are deterministic in their equations: run the same inputs twice and you get exactly the same answer.
  • Randomness enters agriculture, however, through the weather — which is genuinely uncertain.
Takeaway

Distinguish models that produce a single definite output from a given set of inputs (deterministic) from those that incorporate randomness and produce a distribution of possible outputs.

## Teaching Notes Distinguish models that produce a single definite output from a given set of inputs (deterministic) from those that incorporate randomness and produce a distribution of possible outputs (stochastic). Most crop models, including APSIM, are deterministic in their equations: run the same inputs twice and you get exactly the same answer. Randomness enters agriculture, however, through the weather — which is genuinely uncertain. The standard way to handle this is to run a deterministic model across many years of historical weather, generating a distribution of outcomes that captures the real climatic variability of the location. This produces the exceedance probability curves that are far more decision-relevant than a single "expected" yield: a grower cares not just about the average outcome of a nitrogen strategy but about how it performs in the worst two years in ten. ## Teaching Notes Complete the taxonomy with two further axes. Spatial versus non-spatial: a non-spatial (point) model simulates a single homogeneous location — one soil, one weather record, one uniform crop — as most crop models do; a spatial model represents variation across the landscape, either by running a point model separately for each zone or grid cell and aggregating, or by explicitly modelling flows (of water, sediment, nutrients) between locations. This connects directly to precision agriculture, where a paddock is divided into management zones and a point model is run for each. Continuous versus discrete: continuous models represent variables that change smoothly (soil water content, biomass), whereas discrete models represent countable events or states (number of leaves, developmental stage, presence or absence of a pest). Most crop models mix both — continuous state variables updated at discrete daily time steps, with some discrete events (sowing, flowering, harvest) triggered when thresholds are crossed. Common Agricultural Models You Should Know

  • APSIM (Agricultural Production Systems sIMulator), developed by CSIRO and Queensland DPI.
  • DSSAT (Decision Support System for Agrotechnology Transfer) is the widely-used US-originated crop-modelling suite, emphasising model portability across locations.
  • The Wageningen family (SUCROS, LINTUL, WOFOST) established the canonical daily-step soil-plant-atmosphere framework in the Netherlands.
Takeaway

APSIM (Agricultural Production Systems sIMulator), developed by CSIRO and Queensland DPI.

## Teaching Notes Give students a working familiarity with the named models they will encounter in the literature and in practice. APSIM (Agricultural Production Systems sIMulator), developed by CSIRO and Queensland DPI, is the dominant farming-systems model in Australia and the focus of the companion crop-growth chapter and the AGRI4401 practicals; its distinguishing design makes the soil the central shared resource that any crop module can draw on, enabling realistic multi-year rotation simulation. DSSAT (Decision Support System for Agrotechnology Transfer) is the widely-used US-originated crop-modelling suite, emphasising model portability across locations. The Anatomy of a Model: Inputs, Parameters, State and Outputs

  • Inputs (driving variables): the external forcing that changes daily and comes from observation — weather, management events (sowing, fertiliser, irrigation).
  • Parameters: the fixed coefficients that characterise a particular soil, crop variety, or process — radiation-use efficiency, phyllochron, soil water-holding limits.
  • State variables: the quantities the model tracks through time and updates each step — soil water in each layer, crop biomass, leaf area index, soil nitrogen pools.

## Teaching Notes Present the non-negotiable discipline that separates credible modelling from wishful thinking. Calibration is the process of adjusting a model's parameters so that its outputs agree with observed data for a specific site, crop, and set of seasons. Validation is the entirely separate step of testing the calibrated model against independent data — different years, sites, or varieties not used in calibration. Stress the cardinal rule: a model that reproduces the data it was calibrated on has demonstrated nothing about its predictive power; only agreement with independent data constitutes validation. A model tuned to fit one dataset perfectly but failing on new data is overfitted and worthless for extrapolation. Introduce the common quantitative measures of agreement — root mean square error (RMSE), its normalised form (CV-RMSE, ideally under about 15% for yield), bias, and the correlation between predicted and observed — and warn that good aggregate statistics can still hide systematic failures in particular conditions. ## Teaching Notes Teach students to reason about where a model's uncertainty comes from and how far to trust its predictions. Sources of uncertainty: input uncertainty (imperfect weather or soil data), parameter uncertainty (coefficients measured with error or borrowed from elsewhere), and structural uncertainty (processes the model simplifies or omits entirely — biotic stresses, spatial heterogeneity, extreme events). Introduce sensitivity analysis as the tool for identifying which inputs and parameters most influence the output, and therefore which are worth measuring carefully: for WA dryland wheat, the soil's plant-available water capacity and the seasonal rainfall dominate, so effort spent characterising those pays off far more than precision in minor parameters. Explain the practical consequence: a model's prediction should always be presented with an honest statement of its uncertainty and its domain of validity, and used to inform judgement rather than to replace it. ## Teaching Notes Close by consolidating the limitations of current crop models and previewing their future, connecting to themes developed across the unit. Key limitations: standard crop models simulate a single homogeneous point and ignore within-paddock spatial variability; they typically omit biotic stresses (weeds, pests, diseases) that are central to real yield loss; they simplify extreme events, waterlogging, and soil constraints; and their accuracy is capped by the quality of their soil and weather inputs.
