Takeaway

Address directly the learning outcome of why cereals — especially wheat and barley — are lower-risk than legumes or canola for growers in the drier parts of the grainbelt.

## Teaching Notes Address directly the learning outcome of why cereals — especially wheat and barley — are lower-risk than legumes or canola for growers in the drier parts of the grainbelt, an idea central to understanding rotation and risk decisions. Lay out the reasons. Establishment reliability: cereals germinate and establish vigorously from a wider range of sowing conditions and marginal breaks than the more finicky pulses and canola, whose small seed and specific requirements make establishment failure more likely in a dry start. Stress tolerance and compensation: cereals tolerate drought, heat, and nutrient stress better and, crucially, compensate for stress through their tillering architecture (developed later in the lecture), so a cereal that suffers early stress can still recover a reasonable yield, whereas canola and pulses are less forgiving. Market depth and price stability: wheat and barley have large, liquid, well-understood markets, whereas pulse and even canola markets are thinner and more volatile. Agronomic maturity: cereal weed, disease and nutrition management is well established and lower-risk. Explain the consequence for rotation: in low-rainfall, high-risk environments growers lean heavily on cereals and use break crops sparingly and strategically, while in higher-rainfall, lower-risk zones the more valuable but riskier break crops can occupy a larger share. ## Teaching Notes Introduce the structure of the cereal plant and the yield-component framework that underlies all cereal agronomy and modelling. Describe the plant: a main stem plus tillers (side shoots), each capable of producing a head (spike/ear) bearing spikelets and florets that set grain; a root system; and leaves culminating in the flag leaf that supplies most of the grain's carbohydrate. Present the multiplicative yield-component chain — plants per unit area × heads per plant (driven by tillering) × grains per head × weight per grain — the framework, traceable to Engledow and Wadham, that lets an agronomist attribute a yield difference to the specific developmental stage at which it was set. ## Teaching Notes Introduce the Zadoks decimal growth scale as the universal language for describing cereal development, essential for timing every management input. Explain the two-digit structure: the first digit denotes the principal growth stage (0 germination, 1 seedling growth, 2 tillering, 3 stem elongation, 4 booting, 5 head emergence, 6 flowering/anthesis, 7 milk development, 8 dough development, 9 ripening) and the second digit subdivides it (for example Z13 = three leaves unfolded, Z30 = start of stem elongation, Z39 = flag leaf fully emerged, Z65 = mid-flowering). Emphasise why a precise, shared staging system matters: nearly every agronomic decision — the timing of herbicides (many of which damage the crop or are ineffective outside a specific window), nitrogen topdressing, plant growth regulators, and fungicides — is specified by growth stage, not by calendar date, because the crop's physiological readiness, not the date, determines the response. Explain that Zadoks stages are observable in the field, allowing an agronomist to walk a crop, determine its stage, and time an input correctly, and that they provide the bridge for comparing field crops against crop-model predictions. Vegetative Development: Germination, Emergence and Tillering

  • Follow the crop through its vegetative phase and the agronomy it demands.
  • Germination and emergence: driven by seedbed moisture and temperature, with sowing depth critical.
  • Establishment sets the plant-number yield component.
Takeaway

Follow the crop through its vegetative phase and the agronomy it demands.

## Teaching Notes Follow the crop through its vegetative phase and the agronomy it demands. Germination and emergence: driven by seedbed moisture and temperature, with sowing depth critical — too deep and the coleoptile (short in modern semi-dwarf varieties) cannot reach the surface, too shallow and the seed risks a drying seedbed. Establishment sets the plant-number yield component. Tillering: from around the three-leaf stage the plant produces tillers, each a potential head; tiller number responds strongly to conditions — good moisture, nitrogen, light (low density) and warmth promote tillering, while stress suppresses it. This is the first great compensation mechanism: a thin or stressed early crop tillers more to fill the canopy, while a thick or vigorous crop produces fewer tillers per plant, so head number is buffered across a wide range of plant densities. Explain the agronomic implications: early nitrogen and weed control protect tillering and canopy establishment, and the flexibility of tillering is a major reason cereals tolerate variable establishment far better than the non-tillering break crops. Takeaway

## Teaching Notes Cover the reproductive phase, when the yield-critical grain-number component is set and the crop is most sensitive to both stress and mis-timed management. Stem elongation (Z30 onward): the growing point, previously protected at ground level, rises up the stem, and from this point the developing head is vulnerable to frost and to certain herbicides — making Z30 a critical cut-off for many chemicals. The number of grain sites is being determined through this phase. Booting and head emergence (Z40–Z59): the head grows within the flag-leaf sheath and emerges. Flowering/anthesis (Z60–Z69): the yield-defining event, when florets are pollinated and grain number is finally set. Emphasise that the period around flowering is the crop's most vulnerable: frost can sterilise florets, heat above ~35 °C damages pollen, and water or nutrient stress reduces the grain number that can be supported — and these losses are largely irreversible because grain number, the most important yield component, is fixed here. This is why matching flowering to the safe window (between frost and heat/terminal drought) via variety and sowing date, and protecting the crop through this phase, are the highest priorities in cereal agronomy. Takeaway

Complete the developmental sequence with grain fill and maturity, when grain size — the final yield component — and much of grain quality are determined.

## Teaching Notes Complete the developmental sequence with grain fill and maturity, when grain size — the final yield component — and much of grain quality are determined. After flowering, the grain accumulates starch and protein through the milk (Z70s) and dough (Z80s) stages, drawing on current photosynthesis from the flag leaf and upper canopy plus remobilised reserves stored in the stem before flowering. Grain size is set by the balance between the supply of assimilate and the duration of grain fill. Explain the dominant WA reality: in the Mediterranean environment grain fill almost always runs into the terminal drought and rising heat, so the fill period is truncated and grain is frequently smaller than its genetic potential — producing the pinched, screened grain of a dry finish and reducing both yield and quality. Discuss the physiology this creates: pre-flowering stem reserves become a crucial buffer that sustains grain fill when the canopy fails under terminal stress, and a good finishing rain can lift grain size and test weight markedly. Maturity (Z90s) is reached when the grain dries down to harvestable moisture. Genotype × Environment × Management

## Teaching Notes Develop the crucial concept of stress compensation — the buffering that makes cereals so resilient and lower-risk than break crops — by tracing how the sequentially-set yield components offset one another. Because plant number, head number, grain number, and grain size are determined at successive stages, stress that reduces one component can be partly compensated by the components set later, provided conditions improve. A crop thinned by poor establishment tillers more, restoring head number. A crop stressed during tillering may still set adequate grains per head if conditions ease by flowering. A crop with reduced grain number can produce larger grains if the finish is kind. Explain that this compensation operates through the crop's responsiveness to resource availability at each stage — the same tillering and grain-set flexibility that a crop model represents through its source-sink and stress-factor logic. Stress the agronomic significance: this is why early stress is rarely fatal to cereal yield and why cereals recover from setbacks that would ruin a canola or pulse crop, and it is the physiological basis for the lower risk of cereals in marginal environments. ## Teaching Notes Return to water-use efficiency, introduced in the opening lecture, and apply it specifically to comparing and improving cereal performance. Restate the French-Schultz framework: potential yield rises with growing-season water use above an evaporation threshold, at a slope representing the crop's transpiration efficiency, and the gap between a paddock's actual yield and this water-limited potential reveals the scope for agronomic improvement. Use WUE as a diagnostic and comparison tool: a crop achieving close to its water-limited potential is well managed for that season, while a large yield gap points to a correctable constraint — weeds competing for water, disease or nutrient deficiency limiting canopy function, subsoil acidity or compaction restricting root access to water, or poorly matched phenology wasting the season. ## Teaching Notes Cover barley quality and the distinctive malting-versus-feed decision that shapes its agronomy. Explain that barley is grown for two very different markets: malting barley, which must meet exacting specifications for the brewing and distilling industry and commands a significant premium, and feed barley, which has looser specifications and a lower price. ## Teaching Notes Conclude by drawing together the whole lecture into an integrated understanding of how the three influences — agronomy, soils, and climate — jointly determine both cereal yield and grain quality, reinforcing the G × E × M theme. Climate sets the envelope: growing-season rainfall and its reliability cap yield through water supply, temperature extremes at flowering and grain fill impose frost and heat penalties and shape grain size and quality, and the terminal drought that ends most WA seasons is the dominant influence on final grain plumpness and screenings.
