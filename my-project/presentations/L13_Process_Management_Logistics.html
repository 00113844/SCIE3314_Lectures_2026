## Teaching Notes Develop the central operational concept introduced in the economics lecture: timeliness, and the management of capacity to achieve it. Explain that because each field operation has an agronomically optimal window (sowing soon after the break, spraying at the right growth stage, harvesting before weather damage), the farm's capacity to complete each operation across its whole area within that window directly determines yield, quality, and cost — the timeliness principle. Introduce the idea of the bottleneck: in any sequence of operations, the stage with the least capacity relative to demand limits the throughput of the whole system, so a farm's effective capacity is set by its tightest constraint (for example, if grain cannot be carted away from the harvester fast enough, the header sits idle regardless of how fast it can thresh). ## Teaching Notes Introduce harvest as the culminating and highest-stakes operation of the season, where a year's work and investment are finally realised as grain in the bin — or lost. ## Teaching Notes Examine the harvester (header) and the sources of harvest loss it must minimise. Describe the harvester's task at a functional level: cutting or gathering the crop, threshing the grain from the plant, separating grain from straw and chaff, and cleaning it, before delivering clean grain to the tank and discharging residue — increasingly with weed-seed destruction integrated at the residue exit (harvest weed seed control from the weeds lecture). Explain the main sources of harvest loss and their management: front (header) losses, where grain is shed at the cutting front before it enters the machine (the largest loss source in shatter-prone crops like canola, managed by windrowing, shatter-tolerant varieties, and careful front set-up); and threshing, separation, and cleaning losses inside the machine, where grain is lost out the back with the straw if the machine is overloaded or poorly adjusted. Explain the trade-off between capacity and loss: driving faster lifts throughput but increases losses, so there is an optimal ground speed that balances the value of getting the crop off quickly against the grain lost — a real-time process-management decision. Emphasise the importance of correct machine set-up and adjustment for the crop and conditions (which change through the day as moisture and crop condition change), and of monitoring losses, since harvest losses are invisible unless deliberately measured and can quietly cost a significant share of yield. ## Teaching Notes Explain how grain quality is managed and preserved through harvest, since the operational choices here determine the grade and value of the delivered grain. ## Teaching Notes Describe the grain supply chain that carries grain from the farm to the export port, the physical logistics that connect WA growers to world markets. Explain the structure of the WA system: grain is delivered from farms to a network of country receival sites, where it is sampled, graded, weighed, and segregated by grade, then accumulated and transported (by road and rail) to the export terminals at the ports, from which it is loaded onto bulk ships for export. Explain the distinctive WA context: the WA bulk handling system is dominated by CBH Group, a large grower-owned cooperative that operates the country receival network, storage, and much of the transport and port terminal infrastructure — a grower-controlled supply chain that is a notable feature of the WA industry. Explain the functions the system performs: aggregating the grain of thousands of growers into the large, uniform, quality-assured parcels that export markets and bulk shipping require; segregating grain by grade and quality so that specific specifications can be met and premiums captured; storing grain between harvest and shipment (since harvest is compressed but export is spread across the year); and moving enormous tonnages efficiently to port. ## Teaching Notes Explain what happens when grain is delivered, since the receival process is where the grower's grain is turned into a graded, valued, tradable commodity. Describe the receival process: each load is sampled and tested against the receival standards for its intended grade — measuring the quality parameters from the agronomy lectures (protein, moisture, screenings, test weight, falling number, defects, contamination, and variety) — and assigned to a grade accordingly, or rejected or downgraded if it fails a specification. Explain the consequences for the grower: the grade determines the price, so the quality management of the whole season and the harvest culminates in this grading decision, and a load that misses a specification (too much moisture, too many screenings, low falling number, contamination) is downgraded with a real financial penalty. Explain the system-level function of quality assurance: consistent, tested grading across the receival network gives buyers confidence that a WA grade means the same thing regardless of which grower or site it came from, which is what allows the aggregated bulk parcels to be sold against international specifications — the quality-assurance reputation that is part of WA's market advantage. ## Teaching Notes Examine the transport and storage logistics of the supply chain and the efficiency pressures on them, since these costs and constraints affect the grower's net return and the industry's competitiveness. Explain the transport task: moving very large tonnages from dispersed country sites to a few ports, by a combination of road and rail, in a compressed post-harvest period and then across the shipping year — a major logistical and cost undertaking where efficiency (the right mode, minimising handling, avoiding congestion) directly affects the cost of getting grain to market. Explain the storage task: because harvest is compressed into a few weeks but export shipping is spread across the year, the system must store large quantities of grain, segregated by grade, safely (dry, cool, pest-free) between receival and shipment. Explain the efficiency and cost pressures: supply-chain costs (receival, storage, transport, and port charges) are deducted from the grower's return, so the efficiency of the chain matters to farm profitability, and there is constant pressure to reduce handling, improve throughput, and invest in infrastructure (rail, port capacity, storage) — with debates over the balance between the cooperative bulk system and alternatives such as containerised export and direct grower marketing. ## Teaching Notes Conclude by drawing the process-management and logistics content together and connecting it to the arc of the unit.
