## Teaching Notes Examine annual ryegrass in detail, since understanding its life-cycle is the key to managing it. Describe its biology: a highly competitive, genetically diverse annual grass that germinates with the autumn break (in flushes), grows through winter alongside the crop, and sets abundant seed at crop maturity. Two features make it both dangerous and, paradoxically, manageable. First, its enormous genetic diversity and cross-pollinating breeding system make it extraordinarily prone to evolving herbicide resistance — ryegrass has developed resistance to more herbicide modes of action than almost any weed in the world. Second, and crucially for control, its seed is relatively short-lived in the soil: most ryegrass seed germinates or dies within a few years, so the seedbank has little persistence compared with many weeds. Explain the strategic implication of this short seedbank: if seed set can be prevented for even a few consecutive years, the seedbank can be driven down dramatically — so tactics that stop seed reaching the soil (harvest weed seed control, hay or brown-manure, crop-topping) are especially powerful against ryegrass. ## Teaching Notes Contrast wild radish, the dominant broadleaf weed, whose biology demands a different strategy from ryegrass. ## Teaching Notes Introduce integrated weed management (IWM) as the organising framework for the rest of the lecture and the modern response to herbicide resistance. Define IWM as the deliberate combination of many weed-control tactics — chemical, cultural, physical, and biological — applied across the whole cropping sequence so that weeds are attacked at multiple life-cycle stages and by multiple mechanisms, so that no single tactic bears the whole burden and selection pressure for resistance to any one tactic is reduced. ## Teaching Notes Establish why herbicides are indispensable to modern cropping and why protecting them is a strategic priority. Takeaway

## Teaching Notes Introduce the most basic functional classification of herbicides. Non-selective herbicides kill or damage a broad range of plants — both weeds and crops — and so are used where no crop is present or must be protected: as knockdown treatments before sowing (glyphosate being the pre-eminent example, alongside paraquat), for fallow weed control, and for pre-harvest and inter-row applications where contact with the crop is avoided. Selective herbicides discriminate between crop and weed, killing the weed while leaving the crop unharmed, which is what allows in-crop weed control. Explain the biological basis of selectivity: it arises from differences between crop and weed in how the herbicide is intercepted, absorbed, moved, and — most importantly — metabolised (a tolerant crop breaks the herbicide down to harmless products before it acts, while the susceptible weed cannot), or from differences in the target site the herbicide attacks. This same metabolic basis of selectivity underlies both crop safety and, ominously, one form of resistance (metabolic resistance, developed later). ## Teaching Notes Explain how soil-applied, pre-emergent herbicides work and the factors governing their performance — a class of growing importance as resistance has pushed growers back toward pre-emergents. These herbicides are applied to the soil before or at sowing and act on germinating weed seeds and emerging seedlings, being taken up by the roots or shoots of the emerging weed. Their efficacy depends on the herbicide reaching and persisting in the zone where weed seeds germinate at an active concentration. Explain the factors that affect their behaviour and persistence: soil binding (herbicides bind to clay and organic matter, so binding-prone products are less available and need higher rates on high-organic or clay soils, and can be tied up by stubble before reaching the soil); solubility and mobility (which govern how the herbicide moves with soil water into the germination zone — too little movement and it never reaches the weed, too much and it leaches past or into the crop seed); and degradation (by microbial breakdown, which is faster in warm, moist soils, and by chemical and photo-degradation), which determines how long the herbicide persists and therefore both its length of control and its plant-back risk to following crops. Emphasise the practical consequences: stubble interception can reduce efficacy in retained-stubble systems, persistence creates plant-back constraints that must be tracked in records, and matching product, rate, and placement to soil type and conditions is essential. ## Teaching Notes Explain how foliar, post-emergent herbicides work once weeds have emerged. These are sprayed onto growing weeds and must be intercepted by and retained on the leaf, penetrate the waxy cuticle, and reach their site of action. Distinguish two behaviours. Contact herbicides act at or near the point of contact, killing the tissue they touch (requiring thorough coverage of the whole weed to be effective, since untouched tissue survives — paraquat is the classic example). Systemic (translocated) herbicides are absorbed and moved within the plant through its transport systems to growing points and roots, killing tissue distant from the point of contact (glyphosate and the phenoxies are systemic) — these need less complete coverage but require the weed to be actively growing to translocate the herbicide. Explain the practical implications: coverage requirements differ (fine, complete coverage for contact herbicides; adequate but less exhaustive coverage for systemic), and weed condition matters greatly (a stressed, hardened, or dormant weed absorbs and translocates poorly, so herbicides work best on young, actively growing weeds under good conditions). ## Teaching Notes Explain the concept of herbicide mode of action (MOA) and why herbicides are grouped by it — the single most important idea in resistance management. Define mode of action as the specific biochemical process in the plant that the herbicide disrupts (for example, inhibiting an enzyme essential to amino acid synthesis, blocking photosynthesis, or disrupting cell division or growth-hormone regulation). Explain that herbicides sharing a mode of action attack the same target, so a weed that evolves resistance to one herbicide is typically resistant to all herbicides of that mode of action (cross-resistance) — meaning the meaningful unit for resistance management is the mode-of-action group, not the individual product. This is why herbicides are labelled with a mode-of-action group (in Australia now a numeric group code, part of the global HRAC system), and why the golden rule of resistance management is to rotate and mix modes of action so that no single MOA group bears repeated, sole selection pressure. Give the strategic picture: because ryegrass and wild radish are already resistant to several groups, and new groups are scarce, growers must deliberately diversify the MOA groups they use across the rotation and combine them with the non-chemical tactics of IWM. Herbicide Resistance: Target-Site versus Metabolic

  • Develop the mechanisms of herbicide resistance, since understanding them explains why resistance spreads and how to slow it.
  • Define resistance as the inherited ability of a weed population to survive a herbicide dose that once controlled it.
  • Distinguish the two main mechanisms.
Takeaway

Develop the mechanisms of herbicide resistance, since understanding them explains why resistance spreads and how to slow it.

## Teaching Notes Develop the mechanisms of herbicide resistance, since understanding them explains why resistance spreads and how to slow it. Define resistance as the inherited ability of a weed population to survive a herbicide dose that once controlled it — an evolutionary response to the selection pressure of repeated herbicide use, in which rare naturally-resistant individuals survive, set seed, and come to dominate the population. Distinguish the two main mechanisms. Target-site resistance: a mutation changes the herbicide's target protein so the herbicide can no longer bind and act; it typically confers strong resistance to that mode of action (and to others sharing the target), is often specific, and is the more predictable form. Metabolic resistance: the weed evolves enhanced ability to break the herbicide down before it acts (using the same detoxification systems that give crops their selectivity); this is more insidious because it can confer resistance across multiple, chemically unrelated modes of action simultaneously (including ones the weed has never been exposed to), is harder to predict, and can undermine new chemistry. Explain the sobering implication for WA: annual ryegrass in particular has extensive metabolic and target-site resistance across many MOA groups, so growers cannot rely on finding a new herbicide to solve the problem, and the only durable answer is to reduce total reliance on herbicides through IWM and to protect the remaining effective chemistry by rotating MOA groups and driving the weed seedbank down by non-chemical means. ## Teaching Notes Introduce the central concept of spray application: droplet size, and the fundamental trade-off between efficacy and drift that governs every spraying decision. Explain that a spray is not uniform but a spectrum of droplet sizes, characterised by droplet diameter (measured in microns) and summarised in spray-quality categories (from very fine through fine, medium, coarse, to very coarse and ultra coarse). Explain the trade-off. Small (fine) droplets give excellent coverage — many droplets per unit area, better retention on small or vertical targets, and superior performance for contact herbicides and for difficult targets — but they are highly prone to drift (carried away on wind) and to evaporation before reaching the target, causing off-target damage and lost efficacy. Large (coarse) droplets resist drift and evaporation, landing where intended, but give poorer coverage (fewer, bigger droplets) and can run off or bounce. Explain that the optimum droplet size depends on the target and product: fine droplets for contact herbicides and small weeds needing coverage; coarser droplets for systemic herbicides (which translocate and need less coverage) and, critically, for drift reduction near sensitive areas. ## Teaching Notes Explain how the physical set-up of the spray boom determines whether the chemical is applied evenly across the ground — a major and often overlooked source of poor performance. Describe the boom spray's components: the tank, pump, agitation, filters, pressure regulation and control system, boom with regularly-spaced nozzles, and the plumbing delivering spray to each nozzle. Focus on the geometry of even coverage. ## Teaching Notes Introduce the modern electronic spray technologies that have transformed application, chief among them pulse width modulation (PWM). Explain the limitation of conventional systems that PWM overcomes: in a conventional sprayer, rate is tied to pressure and speed, so slowing down (e.g. on a corner) over-applies and speeding up under-applies, and changing speed changes droplet size. PWM decouples these by rapidly pulsing each nozzle's solenoid on and off many times per second and varying the duty cycle (the proportion of time open) to control flow — so the sprayer can hold a constant pressure (and therefore constant droplet size) while varying application rate with speed, maintain even application through turns and speed changes, and enable individual nozzle control for section control (shutting off nozzles over already-sprayed ground) and even variable-rate and targeted spraying. Takeaway

## Teaching Notes Close the lecture with two factors that determine whether a correctly calibrated, well-targeted spray actually works: adjuvants and weather. Adjuvants are substances added to the spray to improve its performance; survey the main types and their roles — surfactants and wetters that reduce surface tension so droplets spread and penetrate the waxy leaf; oils (crop, mineral, and vegetable-based) that enhance uptake of certain herbicides through the cuticle; and formulation and water-conditioning agents (such as ammonium sulphate to counter hard water that antagonises glyphosate), plus anti-drift, sticker, and compatibility agents. Explain that adjuvants can markedly improve efficacy but also carry risks — the wrong adjuvant or too much can increase crop damage, alter droplet size and drift, or cause incompatibility — so adjuvant choice follows the label and the target. Then address weather, which governs both efficacy and drift. Temperature and humidity: hot, dry conditions stress weeds (reducing uptake and translocation) and increase droplet evaporation and drift, while very cold conditions slow weed metabolism and herbicide activity. Wind: light, steady wind is needed to avoid the calm conditions that cause temperature inversions (in which fine droplets hang and drift far off-target — a major cause of serious drift damage) but strong wind causes immediate physical drift, so there is a safe wind window. Delta-T (a combined temperature and humidity index) is used to judge acceptable spraying conditions. Emphasise the integrating message: effective, responsible spraying combines the right chemistry, correct calibration and nozzle choice, appropriate adjuvant, and suitable weather — and getting all of these right, within the legal framework, is what turns the herbicide in the tank into weed control without off-target harm.
