## Teaching Notes Open the unit by re-framing how students think about a crop. Takeaway

## Teaching Notes Introduce the defining feature of WA broadacre cropping — a Mediterranean-type climate with cool, wet winters and hot, dry summers — and the seasonal rhythm it imposes. Rainfall arrives predominantly from May to October, so crops are sown into the autumn "break" (the first substantial rains, typically late April to June) and grow through winter on stored and in-season rainfall, flower in early spring, and fill grain as the soil dries toward a near-inevitable terminal drought in October–November. Every management input is timed against this rhythm: summer weed control and stubble management before the break; seeding and pre-emergent herbicides at the break; in-crop nitrogen and weed control through winter; fungicides and flowering-time management in early spring; and harvest through the dry summer. Emphasise the concept of the "window" — each operation has an agronomically optimal period, and the art of farming a large area is completing each operation across the whole farm within that window despite weather, machinery, and labour constraints. ## Teaching Notes Establish water as the dominant constraint on WA dryland grain yield and introduce the framework that quantifies it. In a rainfed Mediterranean system with no irrigation, the water available to a crop — growing-season rainfall plus any moisture stored in the soil profile over summer — sets the ceiling on what can be produced. Introduce the crop water balance conceptually: of the total rainfall a paddock receives, only a fraction is transpired productively by the crop; the remainder is lost to soil evaporation, runoff, deep drainage below the root zone, and weeds. The central management goal in a water-limited system is to maximise the proportion of scarce water that passes productively through the crop — the rationale behind summer weed control (conserving stored moisture), early sowing (aligning canopy growth with the cooler, lower-evaporation part of the season), and stubble retention (reducing evaporation and runoff). ## Teaching Notes Introduce water-use efficiency (WUE) as the single most useful benchmark for comparing crop performance in Mediterranean-type environments. Define WUE as grain yield produced per millimetre of water used (kg grain per hectare per mm), and present the French and Schultz (1984) framework developed in South Australia and universally applied across southern Australia. Their model expresses potential yield as a function of growing-season water use, where water use is estimated as growing-season rainfall (April–October) minus a soil evaporation loss of approximately 110 mm before the crop can transpire. The slope of the relationship — the transpiration efficiency — represents the biological potential, historically around 20 kg/ha/mm for wheat and now revised upward toward 22–25 kg/ha/mm with modern varieties and agronomy. Explain the power of the framework: a grower can compare a paddock's actual yield against the water-limited potential for that season's rainfall, and a gap between actual and potential reveals a management or constraint problem (weeds, disease, nutrition, subsoil constraints, poor variety) worth diagnosing. ## Teaching Notes Develop how the water-limited yield potential is approached in practice and why most paddocks fall short. The gap between water-limited potential and actual yield is closed from two directions. Genetic improvement: plant breeding has lifted transpiration efficiency, harvest index, and tolerance to frost, heat, and disease, steadily raising the achievable slope of the WUE line over decades. Agronomic improvement: earlier and more reliable sowing, better nutrition, effective weed and disease control, and controlled traffic to reduce compaction all move actual yield closer to potential. Introduce the important idea that yield in a variable climate is not a single number but a distribution — in a good year a paddock may approach its potential, while in a drought year potential itself collapses. This motivates thinking about yield probabilistically (the exceedance curves developed with crop models) and about matching input investment to seasonal outlook — spending on nitrogen in proportion to the water the season is likely to deliver.
