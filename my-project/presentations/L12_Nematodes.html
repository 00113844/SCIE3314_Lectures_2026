## Teaching Notes Open by introducing plant parasitic nematodes as microscopic roundworms that live in the soil and feed on plant roots, causing large but often unrecognised yield losses across the WA grainbelt. Emphasise why they are the "hidden enemy": they are invisible to the naked eye, their damage occurs underground on the roots, and their above-ground symptoms — patchy poor growth, stunting, yellowing, and reduced vigour — are non-specific and easily mistaken for nutrient deficiency, waterlogging, or drought, so their impact is chronically underestimated. ## Teaching Notes Introduce the principal plant parasitic nematodes affecting WA broadacre crops, and their differing habits. Root lesion nematodes (Pratylenchus, particularly P. neglectus and P. thornei / P. quinisulcatus) are the most widespread and economically important in WA cereals: they are migratory endoparasites that move through and feed within the root tissue, killing cells and creating lesions, and they have wide host ranges that make them difficult to starve out. Cereal cyst nematode (Heterodera avenae) is a sedentary endoparasite historically damaging in cereals: the female swells and forms a protective cyst full of eggs that persists in the soil, and it is more host-specific. Root knot nematodes (Meloidogyne species) cause characteristic galls (knots) on roots and affect a range of crops. Stem nematode and others occur in particular situations. Emphasise the key management-relevant contrasts between these groups: host range (wide-host lesion nematodes are far harder to manage by rotation than host-specific cyst nematodes), feeding habit (migratory versus sedentary), and persistence (cyst-forming species survive long in soil). The Nematode Lifecycle

  • eggs hatch to release juveniles, which pass through several moults to become adults; the juveniles and adults feed on roots, and the females produce the next generation of eggs.
  • Reliance on a living host: nematodes need host roots to feed and reproduce, so their populations build up under susceptible crops and decline when a non-host or resistant crop starves them.
  • Survival between hosts: nematodes persist through the summer and non-host periods as eggs (and, for cyst nematodes, within the protective cyst).
Takeaway

## Teaching Notes Explain the general lifecycle of plant parasitic nematodes, since understanding it is the key to their management. Describe the stages common to the group: eggs hatch to release juveniles, which pass through several moults to become adults; the juveniles and adults feed on roots, and the females produce the next generation of eggs, completing the cycle. Explain the lifecycle features that matter for management. Reliance on a living host: nematodes need host roots to feed and reproduce, so their populations build up under susceptible crops and decline when a non-host or resistant crop starves them — the biological basis for rotational control. Survival between hosts: nematodes persist through the summer and non-host periods as eggs (and, for cyst nematodes, within the protective cyst), so populations carry over between seasons and cannot simply be eliminated in one break. Multiple generations and rapid multiplication under a good host: populations can build quickly when a susceptible crop is grown repeatedly. ## Teaching Notes Explain how nematode problems are diagnosed, a crucial step because the symptoms are non-specific and management depends on identifying the species and its population level. Describe the diagnostic pathway. Recognising the field signs: patchy, non-specific poor growth, stunting and yellowing, and poor root systems, often in patches, that do not respond to nutrition — a prompt to investigate rather than a diagnosis in itself. ## Teaching Notes Set out the integrated management of plant parasitic nematodes, which — like weed and disease management — relies on combining tactics that exploit the pest's biology, since chemical options are limited in broadacre cropping. Resistant and non-host crops and varieties: the cornerstone of management — growing crops and varieties that the nematode cannot reproduce on (resistance) starves the population down, and this depends entirely on knowing the species, because a crop that is a non-host to one nematode may be a good host to another. Distinguish resistance (limits nematode multiplication, reducing the population for following crops) from tolerance (the crop yields well despite nematode presence but may still allow the population to build) — the ideal is a variety that is both. Rotation: sequencing resistant and non-host crops to run the population down, which is straightforward for host-specific nematodes (cereal cyst nematode) but difficult for wide-host lesion nematodes that few crops starve. Managing other hosts: controlling weeds and volunteers that host the nematode over the summer and in break phases, since these sustain the population. Maintaining good crop vigour and nutrition: helping the crop tolerate damage, though this does not reduce the population. Note the very limited role of nematicides in broadacre grain (uneconomic and largely unavailable at field scale), which throws the emphasis onto the biological and rotational tactics. Suppressive Soils and the Soil Biology

  • Define a suppressive soil as one in which a pest or pathogen fails to establish or cause damage despite the presence of the pest, a susceptible host, and favourable conditions.
  • a diverse and active soil microbial and faunal community can compete with, parasitise, or prey upon nematodes and pathogens, keeping their populations and damage below threshold.
  • practices that build soil organic matter and biological activity and diversity — stubble retention, reduced tillage, diverse rotations, and organic inputs.
Takeaway

Define a suppressive soil as one in which a pest or pathogen fails to establish or cause damage despite the presence of the pest, a susceptible host, and favourable conditions.

## Teaching Notes Introduce the concept of disease- and nematode-suppressive soils, an area of growing interest that links nematode and disease management to soil biology. Define a suppressive soil as one in which a pest or pathogen fails to establish or cause damage despite the presence of the pest, a susceptible host, and favourable conditions — because the soil's biological community (beneficial microbes, predators, and antagonists) suppresses it. The Rhizoctonia Pathogen Cycle and Pathogenic Variability

  • Rhizoctonia solani is a soil-inhabiting fungus that survives in soil and on organic matter as hyphae and resting structures, without needing a living host between crops.
  • the fungus persists in the soil, colonises crop residues and roots, attacks the new crop's roots early in the season causing the bare patches, and builds inoculum for subsequent crops.
  • pathogens are variable populations that evolve and differ in host range and virulence (as seen with blackleg resistance groups in canola and herbicide resistance in weeds).
Takeaway

Rhizoctonia solani is a soil-inhabiting fungus that survives in soil and on organic matter as hyphae and resting structures, without needing a living host between crops.

## Teaching Notes Examine the management of Rhizoctonia and, importantly, why it is so difficult — a candid treatment of the challenges the learning outcomes emphasise. ## Teaching Notes Broaden to the principles of fungicide use, since fungicides are a key tool against many crop diseases (though limited against soilborne ones like Rhizoctonia). Explain that fungicides, like herbicides, work by disrupting specific biochemical processes in the target fungus, and are grouped by mode of action accordingly. Distinguish the broad functional categories: protectant (contact) fungicides that must be present on the plant surface before infection to prevent the fungus establishing, and systemic fungicides that are taken up and moved within the plant and can act after infection has begun, giving some curative activity. Explain the different modes of action — groups that inhibit different fungal processes (such as sterol biosynthesis, respiration, and others) — and the critical management implication that exactly parallels herbicides: fungi evolve resistance to fungicides, and fungicides sharing a mode of action are subject to cross-resistance, so modes of action must be rotated and mixed to preserve their effectiveness.
