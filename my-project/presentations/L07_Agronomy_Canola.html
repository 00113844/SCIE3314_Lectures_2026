## Teaching Notes Open with the origin story that defines the crop, because canola is a product of plant breeding in a way no other broadacre crop is. Why Grow Canola? The Break-Crop Value

  • As a cash crop: canola is a high-value oilseed sold into strong, growing markets — edible oil and high-protein meal for feed, and increasingly biodiesel feedstock.
  • As a break crop: canola is a broadleaf, non-host to the major cereal root diseases (take-all, crown rot) and delivers a powerful disease break that lifts the yield of the following cereal.
  • It also leaves a residue that decomposes readily and can improve soil structure and water infiltration.
Takeaway

As a cash crop: canola is a high-value oilseed sold into strong, growing markets — edible oil and high-protein meal for feed, and increasingly biodiesel feedstock.

## Teaching Notes Explain canola's dual value proposition that drives its place in the rotation. As a cash crop: canola is a high-value oilseed sold into strong, growing markets — edible oil and high-protein meal for feed, and increasingly biodiesel feedstock, particularly for the European Union where sustainability certification earns premiums. As a break crop: canola is a broadleaf, non-host to the major cereal root diseases (take-all, crown rot) and delivers a powerful disease break that lifts the yield of the following cereal; critically, it is a non-grass crop, so it permits the use of grass-selective herbicides and herbicide-tolerance systems that clean up grass weeds (especially annual ryegrass) which cannot be selectively controlled in a cereal — making canola a cornerstone of integrated weed management. a small seed germinating to a rosette of leaves, then bolting to produce a branched flowering structure bearing many yellow flowers that set pods (siliques).

## Teaching Notes Introduce canola's growth habit and how it forms yield, contrasting it with cereals to explain its different agronomy and risk profile. Describe the plant: a small seed germinating to a rosette of leaves, then bolting to produce a branched flowering structure bearing many yellow flowers that set pods (siliques), each containing small oil-rich seeds. Explain yield formation through its components — plants per unit area, pods per plant (driven by branching and flower number), seeds per pod, and seed weight — and note that, like cereals with tillering, canola compensates through branching, producing more branches and pods per plant at low density. Stress the critical difference from cereals: canola's small seed makes establishment far more sensitive to sowing depth, seedbed moisture, and conditions, so establishment failure is a real risk in a dry start; and its long flowering period, while offering some buffering, coincides with the spring window where heat, moisture stress, and frost can all damage pod and seed set. Explain the oil dimension: because the harvested product is oil-rich seed, and oil synthesis is energetically costly, canola's yield per unit of intercepted radiation, expressed as seed, is lower than a cereal's — the physiological reason canola yields fewer tonnes per hectare than wheat but at a higher price per tonne. ## Teaching Notes Set out the key objectives of modern canola breeding programs, since the choice among canola types is one of the most important decisions in the crop's agronomy. Quality: maintaining the defining low erucic acid and low glucosinolate status, and increasingly breeding for higher oil content (directly priced) and specialty oil profiles (such as high-oleic oils for food processing). Yield and vigour: raising yield potential and early vigour for reliable establishment, with hybrid breeding delivering strong hybrid vigour (heterosis) for establishment, biomass, and yield. Phenology: matching maturity to different sowing windows and environments, including the development of longer-season winter canola types for early sowing and grazing. Disease resistance: above all resistance to blackleg, canola's most damaging disease, plus sclerotinia tolerance. Herbicide tolerance: engineering or selecting tolerance to specific herbicides to enable in-crop grass and broadleaf weed control — the trait that defines the canola "types" and drives the weed-management value of the crop. Stress that these objectives interact and trade off (a high-yielding hybrid may cost more in seed; a herbicide- tolerance trait may carry a small yield penalty), and that variety choice integrates yield, maturity, disease resistance, herbicide-tolerance system, and seed cost. Part 1 · Core idea

Triazine-Tolerant (TT) Canola

  • the triazine-tolerance trait reduces photosynthetic efficiency, so TT varieties carry an inherent yield penalty (commonly around 10–20%) relative to non-TT types of equivalent breeding.
  • the value of robust, affordable weed control on weedy paddocks, and the ability to use canola as a grass-weed clean-up crop, often outweighed the yield cost.
  • Note the environmental consideration — triazine residues and their management — and the trend, with the arrival of newer systems and higher-yielding hybrids.
Takeaway

## Teaching Notes Describe the herbicide-tolerance systems that define canola types, beginning with triazine-tolerant (TT) canola, historically the dominant type in WA. Explain that TT canola carries a naturally-derived tolerance to triazine herbicides (such as atrazine and simazine), allowing these cheap, effective, residual herbicides to be sprayed over the crop to control a broad spectrum of grass and broadleaf weeds — a major weed-control benefit in the weed-pressured WA system. ## Teaching Notes Cover the other axes of canola variety choice beyond herbicide tolerance. Hybrid versus open-pollinated: hybrid canola exploits hybrid vigour for stronger early establishment (valuable given canola's establishment risk), more biomass, better blackleg resistance packages, and higher yield, but seed is more expensive and cannot be retained for resowing; open-pollinated (including many TT) varieties have cheaper, retainable seed but generally lower vigour and yield. The trend in WA is strongly toward hybrids as their yield and vigour advantages, particularly in Roundup Ready systems, justify the seed cost. Takeaway

Focus on establishment, the highest-risk phase of canola agronomy and the one that most distinguishes it from cereals.

## Teaching Notes Focus on establishment, the highest-risk phase of canola agronomy and the one that most distinguishes it from cereals. Explain the challenge: canola's very small seed carries little reserve, so it must be sown shallow (typically just 1–2 cm) into moist soil, making it acutely dependent on seedbed moisture and vulnerable to a drying surface, crusting, and pests (slugs, lucerne flea, redlegged earth mite, and aphids) that can destroy a seedling crop. Discuss the management responses: precise shallow sowing with good seed-soil contact; sowing into moisture on or soon after the break; choosing vigorous hybrids; insecticidal seed dressings and monitoring for establishment pests; and appropriate plant density (canola compensates well through branching, so target densities are modest, but a minimum plant stand is needed for reliable canopy closure and weed competition). Explain the strategic implications: because establishment is the make-or-break step and depends on an uncertain break, canola is concentrated in more reliable rainfall zones, and growers increasingly dry-sow canola to be ready to capitalise on the first rains. ## Teaching Notes Cover the reproductive and harvest phases, where canola's agronomy again differs sharply from cereals. Flowering and pod set: canola flowers over an extended period, offering some buffering against a single stress event, but the spring flowering and pod-fill window is exposed to heat, moisture stress, and frost, all of which reduce pod and seed number and oil content; the crop's long flowering also makes it attractive to pollinators though it is self-fertile. Harvest presents a distinctive problem: canola pods mature unevenly and, when ripe, are prone to shattering (splitting and dropping seed) and to pod loss in wind, threatening large harvest losses. Explain the two harvest methods that address this. Windrowing (swathing): cutting the crop at an intermediate maturity and laying it in windrows to cure and dry evenly, then threshing the windrows with a pickup front — reducing shattering loss and evening out maturity, long the standard WA practice. Direct heading: harvesting the standing crop directly at full maturity, cheaper and simpler and increasingly used with shatter-tolerant (pod-shatter-resistant) hybrid varieties and pod-sealant sprays that reduce shattering risk.
