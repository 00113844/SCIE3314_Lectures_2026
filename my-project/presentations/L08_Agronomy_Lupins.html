What Are Pulses, and Why Do They Matter?

  • For human health: pulses are among the richest plant sources of protein, dietary fibre, and micronutrients, low in fat, and central to healthy and sustainable diets worldwide.
  • For the farming system: pulses fix atmospheric nitrogen through symbiosis with rhizobia bacteria.
  • much of a pulse crop's value is delivered to the following cereal through nitrogen and disease benefits, so pulses must be valued across the rotation, not on their own gross margin alone.
Takeaway

For human health: pulses are among the richest plant sources of protein, dietary fibre, and micronutrients, low in fat, and central to healthy and sustainable diets worldwide.

## Teaching Notes Open by defining pulses (grain legumes) as leguminous crops grown for their protein-rich edible seed — lupins, field peas, chickpeas, lentils, faba beans, and others — and establishing their dual importance to human nutrition and to the farming system. For human health: pulses are among the richest plant sources of protein, dietary fibre, and micronutrients, low in fat, and central to healthy and sustainable diets worldwide, with demand rising as populations grow and plant-protein consumption increases. For the farming system: pulses fix atmospheric nitrogen through symbiosis with rhizobia bacteria, reducing the fertiliser nitrogen requirement of the following crop and building system nitrogen; they provide a disease and grass-weed break that lifts subsequent cereal yields; and they diversify the crop mix and income. Emphasise the systems accounting theme from earlier lectures: much of a pulse crop's value is delivered to the following cereal through nitrogen and disease benefits, so pulses must be valued across the rotation, not on their own gross margin alone. Takeaway

Legumes form a symbiotic partnership with soil bacteria (rhizobia) housed in root nodules: the bacteria fix atmospheric nitrogen gas into ammonia the plant can use.

## Teaching Notes Explain the symbiosis that gives legumes their unique place in the system, since it is the biological foundation of the pulse rotation. Legumes form a symbiotic partnership with soil bacteria (rhizobia) housed in root nodules: the bacteria fix atmospheric nitrogen gas into ammonia the plant can use, and the plant supplies the bacteria with carbohydrate. This lets a legume meet much of its own nitrogen need from the air and leave a nitrogen legacy — in residues, roots, and reduced soil nitrogen depletion — that benefits the next crop, commonly worth a substantial fertiliser-nitrogen credit to a following cereal. Explain the practical requirements for effective fixation: the correct rhizobial strain must be present, so seed is inoculated with the appropriate rhizobia (each legume needs its specific strain), and inoculation is essential when a legume is grown in a paddock for the first time or after a long break; adequate soil conditions (pH, moisture, and absence of toxicity) are needed for nodulation; and high available soil nitrogen suppresses fixation, so legumes fix most when soil nitrogen is low. Takeaway

## Teaching Notes Address directly the major constraints to growing pulses in WA, explaining why — despite their clear rotational value — they remain a minority of the cropped area and their adoption is uneven. Establishment and early vigour: many pulses establish less reliably than cereals and compete poorly with weeds when young. Weed control: as broadleaf crops, pulses (except through specific tolerance systems) have limited selective herbicide options for broadleaf weeds, making weed management difficult and expensive. Disease: pulses are susceptible to a range of fungal diseases (ascochyta blights, botrytis, rusts, root rots) that can devastate a crop in a wet season and demand fungicide programs and careful rotation. Harvest difficulty: many pulses set pods low on the plant, lodge, or shatter, causing harvest losses and requiring careful technique; their seed is also more easily damaged. Market thinness and price volatility: pulse markets are smaller and more volatile than cereal markets, and quality specifications (seed size, colour, defect tolerances for human-consumption markets) are demanding. Sensitivity to season: pulses are generally less able than cereals to compensate for stress, so a poor season hurts them more. ## Teaching Notes Introduce the lupin species relevant to WA and their differing roles. Narrow-leafed lupin (Lupinus angustifolius) is by far the most important — the species that built the WA lupin industry — because it is uniquely adapted to the acid, infertile, sandy soils of the grainbelt that suit few other pulses, and modern varieties are sweet (low-alkaloid), non-shattering, and reasonably early. ## Teaching Notes Balance the picture with the disadvantages and the reasons the lupin area has declined from its peak, an important lesson in how crop fortunes shift. Yield and income variability: lupin yields are lower and more variable than cereals, and lupin prices, tied to feed markets, have often been modest, making the single-year economics unattractive relative to cereals or canola in many seasons. ## Teaching Notes Detail the agronomy of growing a lupin crop, beginning with establishment and nutrition. Establishment: lupins are sown early (they benefit from a long season and early sowing lifts yield), at a target density that achieves canopy closure and weed competition, with attention to sowing depth and seedbed. Inoculation: because effective nitrogen fixation depends on the right rhizobia, lupin seed is inoculated with the specific lupin rhizobial strain, especially on paddocks new to lupins — a critical, cheap step that determines whether the crop fixes nitrogen and yields well. Nutrition: as a nitrogen fixer, lupins need little or no fertiliser nitrogen (and high soil nitrogen suppresses fixation), but they do require phosphorus, potassium, and particularly trace elements — lupins are notably sensitive to some deficiencies on the poor sands they grow on, and adequate nutrition supports both the crop and its nodulation. Soil and pH: lupins tolerate acidity better than most crops but still respond to correcting extreme constraints, and they are sensitive to some soil conditions and residual herbicides from the previous crop, so plant-back and paddock selection matter. ## Teaching Notes Cover the in-crop protection program that lupins require. Weed control: because lupins compete poorly early and offer limited selective broadleaf options, weed management leans on pre-emergent herbicides, the grass-selective chemistry that the break crop enables (a key rotational benefit for running down ryegrass), and integrated tactics; weedy lupin crops yield poorly and set weed seed, so clean establishment is vital. Disease: manage anthracnose through clean, tested, disease-free seed and resistant varieties (the primary control, since it is seed-borne), and manage brown spot, grey leaf spot, and root rots through rotation, stubble management, and fungicides where warranted; sclerotinia can affect lupins in wet springs. Viruses: aphid- transmitted viruses (such as cucumber mosaic virus, which is also seed-borne) can reduce yield, managed through clean seed, monitoring aphids, and minimising aphid landing. Pests: establishment pests (mites, lucerne flea, aphids) and, later, aphids and native budworm attacking pods require monitoring and threshold-based control. ## Teaching Notes Complete the lupin story with harvest, quality, and markets. Harvest: modern narrow-leafed lupin varieties have been bred for reduced pod shattering and to hold their pods, improving harvestability, but timely harvest is still needed to minimise loss, and the large, relatively soft seed must be handled to avoid cracking. Quality and markets: lupin grain is valued principally as a high-protein stockfeed for sheep, cattle, pigs, and poultry, and for the aquaculture and intensive-livestock feed industries, with quality judged on protein, cleanliness, and freedom from defect and admixture; a growing and higher-value market is human consumption, where lupin's high protein and fibre and low starch make it a sought-after ingredient in health foods and plant-protein products, demanding higher grain quality and food-grade handling. Emphasise the export dimension and WA's position as a major lupin producer supplying feed and, increasingly, food markets. Conclude by synthesising the lecture's themes: pulses, and lupins in particular, deliver biological nitrogen, disease and weed breaks, and dietary protein that make them valuable to both the farming system and human nutrition, but their higher production risk, narrower adaptation, and thinner markets mean they must be matched carefully to soil and season, managed attentively, and valued across the whole rotation.
