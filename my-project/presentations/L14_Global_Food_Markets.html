## Teaching Notes Give students a picture of how the world's food is actually produced and which crops matter most, providing the factual foundation for the market discussion. ## Teaching Notes Deepen the concept of food security beyond simply producing enough food, giving students a proper understanding of a term used loosely in public debate. Define food security, in the widely-used framing, as all people at all times having physical, social, and economic access to sufficient, safe, and nutritious food for an active and healthy life, and explain its dimensions: availability (is enough food produced and present?), access (can people afford and physically obtain it?), utilisation (is it nutritious, safe, and properly used?), and stability (is the supply reliable over time, or subject to shocks?). Emphasise the crucial insight that food insecurity is often not primarily a production problem but an access and distribution problem — the world produces enough calories in aggregate, yet hunger persists because of poverty, conflict, poor infrastructure, and unstable supply, so producing more is necessary but not sufficient. Explain how international grain trade contributes to food security: it moves food from surplus to deficit regions, buffers local production shortfalls, and stabilises supply — which is why reliable exporters and open, functioning trade are important to global food security, and why export restrictions during crises (which several countries imposed during recent price spikes) can worsen insecurity in importing nations. ## Teaching Notes Explain how the prices WA growers receive are determined, building on the price-taker theme from earlier lectures. Establish that grain is a globally traded commodity whose price is set by the balance of world supply and demand, discovered through international commodity markets and reference exchanges (such as the major wheat futures markets), and transmitted to the WA farm gate through the export chain, adjusted for freight, quality, and the exchange rate. Explain the supply side: total production across the major exporters, carry-over stocks from previous years (the buffer that dampens or amplifies price moves — low global stocks make prices volatile and sensitive to any shock), and the timing of Northern and Southern Hemisphere harvests. ## Teaching Notes Examine the volatility of grain prices — their tendency to swing sharply and unpredictably — since this volatility is a defining feature of the environment WA growers operate in and a threat to global food security. Explain why grain prices are inherently volatile: demand for food staples is relatively inelastic (people must eat regardless of price, so demand changes little as price moves), and supply is inelastic in the short term (it takes a season to grow more) and subject to large weather-driven swings, so when a supply shock hits an inelastic market, price must move a great deal to clear it. Survey the drivers of volatility: weather and climate shocks in major producing regions (droughts, floods, heat); geopolitical events and conflict (dramatically illustrated by the disruption to Black Sea exports and its effect on world wheat and food prices); trade policy shocks (export bans and restrictions, tariffs, and trade disputes, such as the disruption and later restoration of market access for Australian barley); energy prices (which affect fertiliser and fuel costs and biofuel demand); currency movements; and financial and speculative flows in commodity markets. Explain the consequences: for growers, unpredictable returns that make planning and investment difficult and that motivate the risk-management and marketing strategies of the financial lecture; for importing nations, the risk that price spikes cause hunger and political instability, as seen when food-price spikes have contributed to unrest. ## Teaching Notes Explain the market environment Australian growers sell into and how it has changed, connecting the global market to the individual grower's decisions.
